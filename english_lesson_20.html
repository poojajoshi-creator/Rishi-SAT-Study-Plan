Concept 20 / 27SEC — Punctuation
Apostrophes
Apostrophes do exactly two things: show possession and mark contractions. The its/it's and their/they're/there confusions are the most commonly tested apostrophe errors on the SAT.
~1–2 per moduleTarget: 20 secTest: can you expand the contraction?
'
What apostrophes do
Step 1
Use 1 — Possession

Singular nouns: add 's → the student's notebook, the country's history
Plural nouns ending in s: add ' only → the students' notebooks, the countries' borders
Irregular plurals: add 's → children's toys, women's rights
Proper nouns ending in s: add 's → James's car (SAT standard)

Use 2 — Contractions

The apostrophe marks where letters are omitted:
it's = it is / it has    they're = they are    you're = you are
who's = who is    there's = there is    we're = we are
don't = do not    can't = cannot    won't = will not

The four most-tested confusions

its vs it's: "its" = possessive ("the cat licked its paw"). "it's" = it is / it has ("it's been three days").
their vs they're vs there: "their" = possessive ("their coats"). "they're" = they are. "there" = place or filler.
your vs you're: "your" = possessive. "you're" = you are.
whose vs who's: "whose" = possessive. "who's" = who is / who has.

The expansion test: Try expanding the contraction. "It's cold" → "It is cold" ✓. "The dog wagged it's tail" → "The dog wagged it is tail" ✗ — use "its."

⭐
Strategy
Step 2
1
Apply the expansion test every time. Can you expand the word into two words? If yes → contraction (needs apostrophe). If the expansion sounds wrong → possessive (no apostrophe for pronouns; 's for nouns).
2
Remember: possessive pronouns NEVER have apostrophes. Its, their, your, whose, hers, his, ours, theirs — none have apostrophes. Ever.
3
For nouns: singular → 's / plural ending in s → ' only. "The committee's decision" (singular). "The committees' decisions" (plural — multiple committees).
⚠️
Traps
Step 3
Trap 1
Its vs it's
"The company increased it's profits." Expand: "it is profits" — doesn't make sense. Use "its" (possessive). "It's a remarkable achievement." Expand: "It is a remarkable achievement" ✓ — use "it's."
Trap 2
Their/there/they're
"The researchers published there findings." Should be "their" (possessive). Expand test: "they are findings" ✗, "there findings" (location doesn't fit) ✗ → possessive "their" ✓.
Trap 3
Apostrophe for plural
"The 1990's were transformative." Wrong — plurals don't need apostrophes. "The 1990s were transformative." Apostrophes are NEVER used just to make a word plural.
Trap 4
Whose vs who's
"The scientist who's research was cited..." Expand: "who is research" ✗. The word needed is "whose" (possessive relative pronoun). "The scientist whose research was cited..." ✓

Worked examples
Step 4
Example 1 — its vs it'sEasy
The university updated it's admissions policy to place greater emphasis on socioeconomic diversity.
  • A. No change
  • B. its
  • C. their
  • D. it has
B. Expand "it's": "the university updated it is admissions policy" — doesn't make sense. "its" is the possessive pronoun (the university's policy). No apostrophe needed for possessive pronouns.
Example 2 — Singular vs plural possessiveMedium
The three researchers' findings contradicted their colleague's earlier conclusions about the relationship between diet and cognitive decline.
  • A. No change — the sentence is correctly punctuated
  • B. researcher's findings contradicted their colleagues' earlier conclusions
  • C. researchers findings contradicted their colleagues earlier conclusions
  • D. researchers' findings contradicted their colleagues' earlier conclusions
A. "Three researchers'" — plural (three), ends in s, so add just an apostrophe: researchers'. "Colleague's" — singular (one colleague), add 's: colleague's. A is correct. D incorrectly makes "colleague" plural. The context specifies "their colleague" (singular) — one shared colleague whose conclusions they contradict.
Example 3 — whose vs who'sHard
The novelist who's debut was shortlisted for the Booker Prize has spent the last decade writing screenplays.
  • A. No change
  • B. whose debut
  • C. who is debut
  • D. whos debut
B. Expand "who's": "the novelist who is debut" — doesn't make sense. This is a possessive relative pronoun — "whose debut" (the novelist's debut). "Whose" = possessive, no apostrophe. "Who's" = who is / who has. C literally writes out "who is debut" — wrong. D is not a word.

Practice — 5 questions
Step 5
Practice 1
Their are several compelling reasons to reconsider the current policy on urban development.
  • A. No change
  • B. They're are
  • C. Their is
  • D. There are
D. "There are" is the existential construction (there = introductory filler, are = verb). "Their" is possessive — but no noun follows to be possessed here. "They're" = they are — "they are are" makes no sense. "There are several compelling reasons" ✓.
Practice 2
The policy's effectiveness depends on it's implementation across all government departments, not just those directly responsible for environmental protection.
  • A. No change
  • B. its implementation
  • C. it is implementation
  • D. their implementation
B. Expand "it's": "depends on it is implementation" — doesn't work. This is possessive (the policy's own implementation). Possessive pronoun "its" — no apostrophe. Note: "The policy's" correctly uses 's (singular noun possession) while "its" correctly uses no apostrophe (possessive pronoun).
Practice 3
The two companies merger created the largest telecommunications firm in the region.
  • A. No change
  • B. companies's merger
  • C. company's merger
  • D. companies' merger
D. "Two companies" = plural. Plural possessive: add apostrophe after the s → companies'. "Companies's" (B) is never correct. C (company's) = singular — but the sentence says "two companies." A has no apostrophe — wrong for possession.
Practice 4
You're argument would be more convincing if it were supported by recent empirical evidence rather than anecdotal examples.
  • A. No change
  • B. Your argument
  • C. You are argument
  • D. Yours argument
B. Expand "you're": "you are argument would be more convincing" — doesn't work. This needs the possessive "your" (your argument = the argument that belongs to you). No apostrophe for possessive pronouns.
Practice 5 — Hard
The committee reviewed all five candidates applications before making it's final recommendation to the board.
  • A. No change
  • B. candidates' applications before making it's
  • C. candidates' applications before making its
  • D. candidate's applications before making its
C. Two errors: (1) "candidates applications" needs an apostrophe — five candidates (plural) → candidates'; (2) "it's" → expand: "making it is final recommendation" ✗ → possessive "its." C fixes both: candidates' (plural possessive) and its (possessive pronoun). D uses candidate's (singular) — but there are five candidates.

Summary card
Keep this
Apostrophes — Everything you need
Two uses only
Possession (noun's / nouns') and Contractions (it's = it is). Never for plurals (1990s, not 1990's).
The expansion test
Expand the word into two words. If it makes sense → contraction. If not → possessive pronoun (no apostrophe).
Possessive pronouns: no apostrophe ever
its · their · your · whose · hers · his · ours · theirs — NONE of these ever have apostrophes.
Noun possession
Singular: dog's. Plural ending in s: dogs'. Irregular plural: children's. Proper noun ending in s: James's.
