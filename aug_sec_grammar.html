Subject-Verb Agreement

The Core Rule

A singular subject takes a singular verb. A plural subject takes a plural verb. Simple in theory — the SAT makes it hard by hiding the subject behind long phrases.

Pattern 1
Prepositional phrases between subject and verb
Cross out everything between commas or between the subject and verb
The subject of the verb is never inside a prepositional phrase. Strip the phrase away and match the verb to what's left.
Wrong
The impact of rising sea levels on coastal communities are becoming increasingly severe.
Strip "of rising sea levels on coastal communities" → subject = "The impact" (singular) → needs "is"
Correct
The impact of rising sea levels on coastal communities is becoming increasingly severe.
The strip test: Cross out everything between the subject and the verb. What's left? That's your subject. Match the verb to it.
Pattern 2
Neither/nor · Either/or — the closer subject wins
With "neither X nor Y" or "either X or Y," the verb agrees with the subject closer to the verb.
Singular closer subject → singular verb
Neither the senior partners nor the managing director was informed in advance.
"The managing director" (singular) is closer → singular verb "was"
Plural closer subject → plural verb
Neither the director nor the senior partners were informed in advance.
"The senior partners" (plural) is closer → plural verb "were"
Pattern 3
Collective nouns and indefinite pronouns
Collective nouns (team, committee, government, group, audience) are singular on the SAT — they take singular verbs.

Indefinite pronouns: always singular — each, every, everyone, everybody, someone, anyone, nobody, either, neither, one. Always plural — both, few, many, several.
Collective noun — singular
The committee has reached a unanimous decision.
Indefinite pronoun — singular
Each of the students was required to submit an individual report.
"Each" is singular even though "students" follows. Strip "of the students" → subject = "Each" → singular.
Inverted sentences — verb comes before subject
Sometimes the SAT inverts the sentence so the verb comes first. Find the actual subject before deciding.

❌ "There is several reasons for the delay." → "Reasons" is the subject (plural) → ✅ "There are several reasons."

Verb Tense & Consistency

The Core Rule
Tense must be consistent within a passage unless the timeline genuinely changes
The SAT tests whether you can maintain consistent tense throughout a passage. The wrong answers introduce a tense shift that isn't justified by the meaning.
Wrong — unjustified tense shift
The scientists collected samples from the riverbed, analysed them in the laboratory, and are publishing their findings in a peer-reviewed journal.
First two verbs are past tense; "are publishing" shifts to present progressive without reason.
Correct — consistent past tense
The scientists collected samples from the riverbed, analysed them in the laboratory, and published their findings in a peer-reviewed journal.
When tense shifts ARE correct

A tense shift is correct when the passage is genuinely talking about two different time periods.

✅ "Darwin proposed his theory in 1859, and scientists continue to build on his work today." — Past for Darwin, present for current scientists. Both are correct.

The SAT test: Does the passage give you a time-period signal (then, now, currently, today, at the time, since)? If yes, the shift may be justified. If no, keep tense consistent.

Pronoun Agreement & Reference

The Core Rule
A pronoun must agree with its antecedent in number and be unambiguous
Two failure modes: agreement (the pronoun doesn't match in singular/plural) and ambiguous reference (it's not clear which noun the pronoun refers to).
Wrong — agreement error
Each student must submit their essay before midnight.
On the SAT (formal register), "each" is singular → "his or her" or rewrite to avoid. However, note that the SAT now accepts "their" for singular in some contexts — look at the answer choices to determine what the test expects.
Wrong — ambiguous reference
When the scientist met with the director, she explained the methodology in detail.
"She" could refer to either person. The sentence must be rewritten to clarify.
Correct — unambiguous reference
When the scientist met with the director, the scientist explained the methodology in detail.
The collective noun trap with pronouns

Collective nouns (team, company, government, committee) are singular → use "it," not "they."

❌ "The committee published their findings." → ✅ "The committee published its findings."

❌ "The company announced they would expand." → ✅ "The company announced it would expand."

The "this / it / they" vagueness trap
The SAT often tests whether a pronoun has a clear antecedent. If "this," "it," or "they" could refer to more than one noun in the previous sentence, the pronoun is wrong — the answer replaces it with the specific noun.

❌ "Researchers studied both the compound and the catalyst; it proved unstable." → Which was unstable?
✅ "Researchers studied both compounds; the catalyst proved unstable."

Dangling & Misplaced Modifiers

The Core Rule
A modifier must be placed next to — and clearly refer to — the word it modifies
A dangling modifier has no logical subject to attach to. A misplaced modifier is too far from the word it modifies and accidentally describes the wrong noun. Both are fixed the same way: make the modified noun the subject of the main clause.
Dangling modifier — Wrong
Having studied the data carefully, the conclusion was obvious.
"Having studied the data" — who studied it? Not "the conclusion." The modifier dangles.
Fixed — make the doer the subject
Having studied the data carefully, the researchers found the conclusion obvious.
Possessive noun trap — Wrong
Having conducted fieldwork on three continents, the anthropologist's conclusions challenged prevailing theories.
"The anthropologist's conclusions" is a possessive phrase — it's not the anthropologist. The modifier still dangles.
Fixed
Having conducted fieldwork on three continents, the anthropologist challenged prevailing theories with her conclusions.
The modifier test: Find the opening participial phrase (verb-ing / verb-ed at the start of a sentence). Ask: who or what is doing the action in that phrase? That noun must be the subject of the main clause.
The possessive trap — the most common modifier error on the SAT
"The scientist's discovery was remarkable, having spent years in the field." → "The scientist's discovery" is a possessive phrase, not the scientist. The modifier dangles. The fix: "Having spent years in the field, the scientist made a remarkable discovery."

Parallelism

The Core Rule
Items in a list or comparison must have the same grammatical form
When two or more items are connected by "and," "or," "but," or appear in a list, they must all be the same part of speech and the same grammatical structure. This applies to nouns, verbs, phrases, and clauses.
Wrong — mixed forms in a list
The programme requires students to analyse primary sources, constructing evidence-based arguments, and the evaluation of competing historical interpretations.
Three forms: bare infinitive / gerund / noun phrase — not parallel.
Correct — all bare infinitives
The programme requires students to analyse primary sources, construct evidence-based arguments, and evaluate competing historical interpretations.
The shared "to" rule

When a list of infinitives shares a single "to," don't repeat "to" for subsequent items — and don't switch to a gerund either.

✅ "She hopes to travel, work, and study abroad." (not "to study" — "to" is already shared)

✅ "She hopes to travel, to work, and to study abroad." (all explicit "to" = also correct)

❌ "She hopes to travel, work, and studying abroad." (gerund breaks the pattern)

Wrong — comparison not parallel
The results of the new study were more significant than the old study.
Comparing "results" (noun) to "the old study" (noun) — wrong. Must compare results to results.
Correct
The results of the new study were more significant than those of the old study.
Correlative conjunctions — must be parallel
"Both X and Y" / "not only X but also Y" / "either X or Y" / "neither X nor Y" — both sides must be grammatically parallel.

❌ "The policy was both costly and it was controversial." → ✅ "The policy was both costly and controversial." (adj + adj)

❌ "Not only did she win the award, but also recognition." → ✅ "Not only did she win the award, but she also gained recognition."
6

Conciseness & Redundancy

The Core Rule
The correct answer is always the shortest option that preserves the full meaning
On conciseness questions, the SAT asks you to identify redundant, wordy, or repetitive phrasing. The correct answer conveys exactly the same meaning in fewer words — without losing information.
Common redundancy patterns the SAT tests

"The reason... is because" → redundant. "The reason... is that" OR just "because"

"End result" → "result" (results are always at the end)

"Past history" → "history" (history is always in the past)

"Future plans" → "plans" (plans are always for the future)

"In order to" → "to"

"Due to the fact that" → "because"

"At this point in time" → "now"

"In the event that" → "if"

"Adjective + in nature" → just the adjective ("It was harmful in nature" → "It was harmful")

"Prior to" → "before"

Wordy — Wrong
The reason the experiment failed was because of the fact that the temperature controls malfunctioned.
Concise — Correct
The experiment failed because the temperature controls malfunctioned.
Redundant — Wrong
The committee's final end conclusion was that the policy required reform.
Concise — Correct
The committee's conclusion was that the policy required reform.
The conciseness test: If you can delete a word or phrase without changing the meaning, it should be deleted. The correct answer on these questions is almost always the shortest option that makes grammatical sense.

Practice — 10 Questions

Question 1 — Subject-Verb Agreement
The government's approach to regulating artificial intelligence, particularly in areas involving autonomous decision-making and data privacy, _______ been criticised by both industry leaders and civil liberties advocates.
Which choice correctly completes the sentence?
  • A have
  • B has
  • C were
  • D are
Correct: B — "has." Strip the prepositional phrases: "...in areas involving autonomous decision-making and data privacy" → subject = "The government's approach" (singular). Singular subject → singular verb "has been." A ("have") and D ("are") are plural. C ("were") is past tense, wrong here.
Question 2 — Neither/Nor Agreement
Neither the lead researcher nor the junior scientists _______ informed about the change in protocol before the trial began.
Which choice correctly completes the sentence?
  • A was
  • B were
  • C has been
  • D is
Correct: B — "were." Neither/nor: verb agrees with the closer subject. "The junior scientists" (plural) is closer to the verb → plural "were." A ("was") would be correct if the singular "lead researcher" were closer.
Question 3 — Verb Tense
Over the past decade, researchers identified a strong correlation between sleep deprivation and cognitive decline, conducted numerous clinical trials, and _______ their findings in several landmark studies.
Which choice correctly maintains tense consistency?
  • A are publishing
  • B will publish
  • C published
  • D have published
Correct: C — "published." The list uses simple past tense: "identified... conducted... published." A (present progressive), B (future), and D (present perfect) all break the established past tense pattern without justification.
Question 4 — Pronoun Agreement
The advisory board reviewed the proposal and concluded that _______ recommendations were too vague to implement effectively.
Which choice correctly completes the sentence?
  • A their
  • B its
  • C they're
  • D there
Correct: B — "its." "The advisory board" is a collective noun — singular on the SAT → singular pronoun "its." A ("their") is plural. C ("they're") = "they are" — wrong form. D ("there") = location word, wrong entirely.
Question 5 — Dangling Modifier
Having spent three years studying migratory patterns in the Arctic, _______ were considered groundbreaking by the scientific community.
Which choice correctly fixes the modifier error?
  • A the biologist's findings were considered groundbreaking by the scientific community.
  • B the biologist published findings that were considered groundbreaking by the scientific community.
  • C the findings of the biologist were considered groundbreaking by the scientific community.
  • D the scientific community considered the biologist's findings groundbreaking.
Correct: B. "Having spent three years studying..." — who spent the time? The biologist, not the findings. B makes "the biologist" the subject immediately after the modifier. A and C use possessive "biologist's" — still a dangling modifier (the findings didn't do the studying). D puts "the scientific community" as subject — wrong, they didn't do the studying either.
Question 6 — Parallelism
The foundation's annual report highlighted three priorities: expanding access to clean water in rural communities, reducing child mortality rates, and _______ sustainable agricultural practices.
Which choice correctly completes the parallel list?
  • A the promotion of
  • B to promote
  • C promoting
  • D it promotes
Correct: C — "promoting." The list pattern is gerunds: "expanding... reducing... promoting." A (noun phrase) and B (infinitive) break the gerund pattern. D (clause) is entirely wrong in a list of gerunds.
Question 7 — Parallelism (comparisons)
The productivity gains reported in the new study were considerably larger than _______ in previous research.
Which choice correctly completes the sentence?
  • A the previous research
  • B what previous research found
  • C those reported
  • D the gains that researchers previously found in previous research
Correct: C — "those reported." The comparison must be parallel: "productivity gains" compared to "those [gains] reported in previous research." "Those" refers back to gains. A compares gains to the research itself (wrong). B creates a wordy clause. D is correct in meaning but redundant ("previously" + "previous research").
Question 8 — Conciseness
The reason the clinical trial was suspended was _______ the primary investigator had failed to disclose a significant conflict of interest to the oversight committee.
Which choice most concisely completes the sentence?
  • A because of the fact that
  • B due to the fact that
  • C that
  • D on account of the fact that
Correct: C — "that." "The reason... was that [clause]" is the correct and concise structure. "The reason... was because" is redundant. A, B, and D are all wordy alternatives to "that." The SAT consistently rewards the shortest construction that is grammatically correct.
Question 9 — Pronoun Reference (ambiguous)
When the psychologist interviewed the patient, she described her symptoms in unusually precise detail.
Which revision best clarifies the ambiguous pronoun reference?
  • A No change — the sentence is clear.
  • B When the psychologist interviewed the patient, the patient described her symptoms in unusually precise detail.
  • C When the psychologist interviewed the patient, they described the symptoms in unusually precise detail.
  • D When interviewed by the psychologist, she described her symptoms in unusually precise detail.
Correct: B. Original "she" is ambiguous — could be either woman. B replaces "she" with "the patient," clarifying who described the symptoms. A is wrong — the reference IS ambiguous. C uses "they" for two singular people — wrong. D still uses "she" — still ambiguous.
Question 10 — Mixed (hard)
The board of directors, after reviewing the quarterly earnings report and consulting with its legal and financial advisers, _______ that the proposed merger should proceed to the next stage of due diligence.
Which choice correctly completes the sentence?
  • A determined and came to the conclusion
  • B have determined
  • C determined
  • D were determining
Correct: C — "determined." Three issues: (1) Subject = "The board of directors" (collective noun → singular). (2) Conciseness: "determined and came to the conclusion" is redundant — determined means exactly that. (3) Tense: simple past "determined" fits a completed board decision. A is redundant. B ("have determined") is present perfect — shifts tense unnecessarily. D ("were determining") is past progressive — implies the process was ongoing, not a completed decision.

The 6-Rule Grammar Cheat Card

Every grammar rule the SAT tests

1. Subject-Verb Agreement
Strip prepositional phrases. Neither/nor → closer subject wins. Collective nouns → singular. "Each/every/either" → singular.
2. Verb Tense
Stay consistent unless a genuine time-period shift. Look for temporal signals (now, then, today, at the time).
3. Pronoun Agreement
Pronoun = antecedent in number. Collective nouns → "it/its" not "they/their." Vague "this/it/they" → replace with the specific noun.
4. Modifiers
Opening phrase doer = subject of main clause. Possessive nouns ≠ the person. Test: can the phrase logically describe the subject?
5. Parallelism
Lists: all same form (gerund/infinitive/noun). Comparisons: compare like to like. Correlatives (both/and, not only/but also) = both sides parallel.
6. Conciseness
Shortest correct answer wins. "Reason... is because" → redundant. "Due to the fact that" → "because." "In order to" → "to."
