Text Structure
Single Passage
Every sentence in a passage exists for a reason. These questions ask you to name that reason — its function — not describe what the sentence says.
~2–3 questions per module Target: 60 sec per question Rule: WHY not WHAT
🏗
What is Text Structure?
Step 1
Core definition

Text Structure questions ask about why a sentence, example, or paragraph exists within the passage — its purpose, its role, its function. They do NOT ask what the sentence says.

Think of a passage as a building. Each sentence is a structural component — a pillar, a bridge, a foundation stone. A Text Structure question asks: "What architectural role does this piece play?"

Two types of Text Structure questions appear on the SAT:
Type A: "The underlined sentence primarily serves to..." (function of one sentence)
Type B: "Which choice best describes the overall structure of the text?" (function of the whole passage)

This lesson covers Type A. Lesson 3 covers Type B.

The fundamental insight

Every sentence in a passage could have been replaced by a different sentence with the same information but a different function. A Text Structure question is testing whether you can see the function rather than the content.

Example: "The Eiffel Tower is 330 metres tall." This sentence could:
• Introduce a passage about Paris landmarks
• Support a claim that Paris dominates European skylines
• Contrast with a statement about shorter buildings
• Illustrate an abstract point about the scale of 19th-century engineering

The same sentence, four different functions. Your job is to identify which one applies in this passage.

🎯
How it appears on the SAT
Step 2
Question stems you will see

• "The underlined sentence primarily serves to..."
• "The function of the third sentence is to..."
• "In the text, the author mentions X primarily to..."
• "Why does the author include the example of X?"

All of these are asking the same thing: what job does this sentence do within the passage?

What the answer choices look like

Correct answers describe function using action verbs:
"provide evidence," "introduce a counterexample," "illustrate an abstract claim," "complicate the argument," "establish context for..."

Wrong answers describe content:
"explain that the Eiffel Tower is 330 metres tall," "state that Paris has tall buildings," "describe the construction of the tower"

If an answer choice summarises what the sentence says rather than what it does, it is wrong.

⭐
The strategy — WHY not WHAT
Step 3
1
Read the whole passage first. You cannot understand a sentence's function without understanding the passage it sits in. Identify the passage's main argument or topic in one sentence.
2
Ask the deletion test: "What would the passage lose if this sentence were removed?" This forces you to think about function. If the answer is "a concrete example," that's the function. If "evidence for the previous claim," that's the function.
3
Name the function in your own words before looking at choices. Use a verb: "this sentence SUPPORTS the claim above" or "this sentence INTRODUCES a complication."
4
Eliminate any choice that describes content instead of function. If a choice starts with "explain that..." or "state that..." and then summarises the sentence, it's wrong.
5
Check the relationship words. Does the sentence start with "However," "For example," "In contrast," "Additionally"? These signal the function directly. "However" → contrast or complication. "For example" → illustration.
📋
The 8 sentence functions — learn these
Step 4

Every sentence in an SAT passage plays one of these roles. Recognise the role and you can answer the question.

1 — Introduce

Sets up a topic, character, concept, or debate. Usually the first sentence of a passage or paragraph. Establishes what the passage is about. Answer choices use words like: "establishes," "introduces," "presents," "sets up."

2 — Support / provide evidence

Gives data, a fact, or an expert's finding that backs up the claim made in the previous sentence. The supported sentence makes an assertion; this sentence proves it. Look for: statistics, studies, direct quotes, specific facts. Answer choices: "provides evidence," "supports the claim," "corroborates."

3 — Illustrate

Gives a concrete, specific example of an abstract point made above. Different from support: illustration makes an idea tangible, support proves it true. Look for: "for example," "for instance," "as demonstrated by," specific cases or anecdotes. Answer choices: "illustrates," "demonstrates," "gives a concrete example of."

4 — Contrast

Introduces an opposing situation, finding, or view to create a comparison. Look for: "however," "in contrast," "whereas," "unlike," "on the other hand." Answer choices: "contrasts," "sets up a comparison," "highlights a difference."

5 — Complicate

Adds nuance to what came before without directly contradicting it. A "yes, but..." move. The previous claim is still valid — this sentence just makes it messier. Look for: "however," "yet," "although," "nevertheless." Answer choices: "complicates," "qualifies," "adds nuance to," "acknowledges a limitation."

6 — Conclude / synthesise

Draws together the points made earlier into a larger observation or takeaway. Often the final sentence of a passage or paragraph. Answer choices: "draws a conclusion," "synthesises," "generalises from the examples above."

7 — Provide background / context

Gives historical, scientific, or situational context that makes the main point understandable. It doesn't advance the argument — it sets the stage. Answer choices: "provides context," "establishes background," "explains the historical setting."

8 — Qualify / limit

Narrows or limits a broad claim by specifying conditions under which it applies. "This is true — but only when X." Answer choices: "qualifies," "limits the scope of," "specifies conditions under which."

⚠️
The three traps
Step 5
Trap 1
Content description
The answer accurately describes what the sentence says — but that's not what the question asks. It asks what the sentence DOES.
Wrong: "explain that Darwin published On the Origin of Species in 1859." Right: "provide historical context for the debate about evolution."
Trap 2
Wrong function, right content
The answer names a real function (illustrate, support, contrast) but applies it to the wrong thing. "Illustrates the author's main argument" when it actually illustrates a sub-point.
The sentence illustrates a specific example from the 1800s. Wrong answer: "illustrates the passage's overall thesis about scientific progress."
Trap 3
Complicate vs Contrast
These are different. Contrast sets up a comparison of two things. Complication adds nuance to ONE thing. Many students use them interchangeably — the SAT doesn't.
Contrast: "Penguins cannot fly; eagles can." Complication: "Penguins cannot fly, though they are skilled swimmers — so the category of flightless birds is more complex than it appears."
Trap 4
Overstating the scope
Answers that say "introduces the passage's main argument" or "proves the author's overall thesis" when the sentence only does something small and local.
A sentence giving one statistic doesn't "prove the thesis." It "provides evidence for the preceding claim."

Worked examples
Step 6
Example 1 — Illustration vs SupportMedium
Scientific consensus can shift dramatically over relatively short periods. In the mid-twentieth century, the prevailing view held that stomach ulcers were caused by stress and diet; within two decades, Barry Marshall and Robin Warren had demonstrated that most ulcers result from bacterial infection — a finding that earned them the Nobel Prize in 2005. What appears settled in science is often merely the most defensible position given current evidence.
💭 Thinking
Main claim: scientific consensus can shift dramatically. Deletion test: without the underlined sentence, we have a general claim with no evidence. With it, we have a specific historical case. Function: illustrates the general claim with a concrete historical example.
The underlined sentence primarily serves to:
  • A. explain that Barry Marshall and Robin Warren won a Nobel Prize
  • B. illustrate the opening claim about scientific consensus with a specific historical case
  • C. provide evidence that bacteria are the primary cause of illness
  • D. contrast the mid-twentieth century view of medicine with modern understanding
Answer: B. The sentence illustrates the opening claim with a concrete case.

A describes content, not function — Trap 1. C overstates — the sentence isn't about bacteria as primary causes of illness generally. D is tempting because there IS a contrast within the sentence (old view vs new discovery), but the sentence's function relative to the PASSAGE is to illustrate the opening claim, not to set up a contrast.
Example 2 — ComplicationMedium
Remote work has been shown to increase productivity for many employees, with multiple studies documenting improvements in output and job satisfaction since its widespread adoption. However, these gains have not been distributed evenly: workers in smaller living spaces, those with young children at home, and employees who rely on in-person collaboration have consistently reported lower productivity and higher stress levels under remote arrangements.
💭 Thinking
First sentence: remote work increases productivity (positive claim). Underlined sentence starts with "However" and qualifies: yes, but NOT for everyone. This doesn't contradict the first claim — it narrows it. Function: complicates the first claim by showing it doesn't apply uniformly.
The underlined sentence primarily serves to:
  • A. contradict the claim that remote work increases productivity
  • B. explain why remote work should be restricted for certain employees
  • C. complicate the opening claim by showing that productivity gains from remote work are unevenly distributed
  • D. provide evidence that remote work is ineffective overall
Answer: C. The sentence doesn't contradict — it qualifies. The opening claim is still true for many; this sentence shows it's not true for all. That's complication, not contradiction.

A (contradict) is wrong because the sentence says "these gains have not been distributed evenly" — it accepts that gains exist. D (ineffective overall) makes a stronger claim than the text supports. B introduces a policy argument not present in the passage.
Example 3 — Background contextMedium
Before the development of germ theory in the late nineteenth century, physicians had no reliable framework for understanding how infectious diseases spread. It was against this backdrop of profound uncertainty that Joseph Lister introduced antiseptic surgical techniques — an innovation that would reduce post-operative mortality rates dramatically.
💭 Thinking
The passage is really about Lister's contribution. But the first sentence isn't about Lister — it's about the state of medicine BEFORE Lister. Function: provides historical context that makes Lister's innovation understandable and significant. Without knowing how bad things were before, the reader can't appreciate why Lister mattered.
The underlined sentence primarily serves to:
  • A. introduce the main argument about the importance of scientific progress
  • B. explain what germ theory is and how it was developed
  • C. establish the historical context that makes Lister's contribution significant
  • D. argue that pre-germ-theory medicine was dangerous
Answer: C. The sentence sets the stage (profound uncertainty before Lister) so the reader can understand why Lister mattered.

A overstates scope — the passage is specifically about Lister, not scientific progress generally. B describes content — the sentence doesn't explain germ theory, it says physicians lacked it. D implies an argument against pre-germ-theory medicine, but the sentence is neutral historical description.

Practice — 5 questions
Step 7
Practice 1
Urban beekeeping has become increasingly popular in cities worldwide. A 2022 survey of municipal beekeepers in ten major cities found that urban honey yields have increased by an average of 34% over the past decade, driven largely by the expansion of pollinator-friendly planting programmes in public parks and private gardens.
The underlined sentence primarily serves to:
  • A. explain why urban beekeeping has become popular
  • B. support the opening claim with specific data about the growth of urban beekeeping
  • C. argue that public parks should expand pollinator planting programmes
  • D. contrast the productivity of urban and rural beekeeping
B. The opening sentence makes a claim (urban beekeeping is popular and growing). The underlined sentence provides specific data (34% increase, survey of 10 cities) to support it. A describes a reason for popularity — the sentence doesn't explain why. C makes a policy argument not in the text. D introduces a contrast not present.
Practice 2
The English language absorbs vocabulary from other languages at an extraordinary rate. French, Latin, Norse, and Arabic have all contributed thousands of words to modern English. The word "algebra," for example, comes from the Arabic "al-jabr," introduced to Europe through the translations of ninth-century scholar Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi.
The underlined sentence primarily serves to:
  • A. introduce a discussion of the history of mathematics
  • B. explain the origins of the word "algebra"
  • C. illustrate the preceding claim about Arabic's contribution to English with a specific example
  • D. provide evidence that Arabic is more influential than Latin on English vocabulary
C. The sentence begins "for example" — always a signal of illustration. It gives one concrete instance of Arabic's contribution to English vocabulary. B describes content (what the sentence says) not function. D makes a comparative claim not supported by the passage.
Practice 3
Conservation programmes that focus exclusively on charismatic megafauna — lions, elephants, tigers — have been criticised for neglecting less photogenic but ecologically critical species. Dung beetles, for instance, process over 80% of mammalian waste in many African ecosystems; their decline would destabilise nutrient cycles that the entire ecosystem depends upon. Effective conservation, researchers argue, must account for the full web of ecological relationships.
The underlined sentence primarily serves to:
  • A. argue that dung beetles deserve more conservation funding than lions
  • B. illustrate the ecological importance of overlooked species by providing a specific example
  • C. contradict the first sentence's claim about conservation programmes
  • D. provide a transition to the final sentence's conclusion
B. "For instance" signals illustration. The dung beetle example makes concrete the abstract point about ecologically critical but overlooked species. A makes a funding argument not in the text. C — the sentence doesn't contradict, it supports the criticism of megafauna-focused conservation.
Practice 4
Machine learning systems have demonstrated remarkable accuracy in diagnosing certain medical conditions from imaging data. However, researchers have found that many of these systems perform significantly worse when tested on images from patient populations that were underrepresented in the training data — a limitation that raises serious questions about their clinical reliability.
The underlined sentence primarily serves to:
  • A. refute the claim that machine learning is accurate in medical diagnosis
  • B. explain how machine learning systems are trained
  • C. complicate the opening claim by revealing a significant limitation that affects clinical reliability
  • D. contrast machine learning with traditional diagnostic approaches
C. The opening claim (remarkable accuracy) is not refuted — the systems ARE accurate on the populations they were trained on. The sentence adds nuance: accuracy breaks down on underrepresented groups. That's complication, not contradiction. A overstates. D introduces a comparison not in the passage.
Practice 5 — Hard
The photograph is often assumed to be a transparent record of reality — a mechanical trace of what was actually there. Yet every photograph reflects choices: the angle of the camera, the moment of the shutter, the cropping in the darkroom, the caption written by an editor who may never have been at the scene. To treat photographs as unmediated evidence is to misunderstand the medium entirely.
The underlined sentence primarily serves to:
  • A. explain the technical process of developing a photograph
  • B. introduce the concept of documentary photography
  • C. challenge the assumption in the first sentence by demonstrating that photographs involve multiple layers of human decision-making
  • D. provide evidence that photographic captions are unreliable
C. The first sentence states a common assumption (photographs = transparent record). "Yet" signals the challenge coming. The underlined sentence lists all the choices involved in making a photograph — directly challenging the "transparent record" assumption. A describes content. D focuses on one element (captions) and claims it's unreliable, which overstates.
