Concept 18 / 27SEC — Punctuation
Semicolons
A semicolon is a period in disguise. The rule is absolute: a complete sentence must appear on BOTH sides. Nothing else.
~1–2 per moduleTarget: 25 secRule: complete sentence on BOTH sides
;
What semicolons do
Step 1
The single rule

A semicolon must have a complete independent clause on BOTH sides. It functions exactly like a period — the two clauses could be separate sentences.

✓ "The results were unexpected; the team revised their hypothesis."
✓ "She won the award; her colleagues were delighted."
✗ "The results; which were unexpected" — "which were unexpected" is not a complete sentence
✗ "She won the award; a significant achievement" — "a significant achievement" is a phrase, not a sentence

Two uses of semicolons

Use 1 — Joining related independent clauses:
"The economy grew by 3%; unemployment fell to a forty-year low."

Use 2 — Separating complex list items that contain commas:
"The delegation included representatives from Paris, France; London, England; and Berlin, Germany."
This prevents confusion between the commas within items and the commas separating them.

⭐
Strategy
Step 2
1
Test both sides. Read before the semicolon: complete sentence? Read after the semicolon: complete sentence? Both must be yes. If either is no → semicolon is wrong.
2
Remember: transitional adverbs need a semicolon before them. "Clause; however, clause" ✓. "Clause, however, clause" ✗ (comma splice). The semicolon goes BEFORE the transitional adverb.
3
Never put a coordinating conjunction after a semicolon. "Clause; but clause" is wrong — use either a semicolon alone OR a comma + but. Not both.
⚠️
Traps
Step 3
Trap 1
Semicolon before a phrase
"The study was significant; a landmark in the field." — "a landmark in the field" is a noun phrase, not a sentence. Wrong. Use a colon or a dash instead.
Trap 2
Semicolon + FANBOYS
"She studied; but she still failed." — semicolons don't combine with coordinating conjunctions. Use "She studied, but she still failed." or "She studied; she still failed."
Trap 3
Semicolon before a relative clause
"The experiment; which took three years, produced remarkable data." — "which took three years" is a relative clause, not an independent clause. The semicolon is wrong here — remove it or use a comma.

Worked examples
Step 4
Example 1Easy
The coastal town had been devastated by the hurricane _______ recovery efforts began within days of the storm's passing.
  • A. hurricane, recovery
  • B. hurricane; recovery
  • C. hurricane: recovery
  • D. hurricane, and recovery
B. Both sides are complete sentences. The semicolon joins them. A = comma splice. C = colon — the recovery effort doesn't explain the devastation, it follows from it; semicolon is more appropriate. D = comma + "and" works but is less tight than a semicolon.
Example 2 — Semicolon before a phrase (error)Medium
The proposal addressed three separate issues; funding, staffing, and long-term sustainability.
  • A. No change
  • B. issues, funding
  • C. issues: funding
  • D. issues. Funding
C. "Funding, staffing, and long-term sustainability" is a list, not an independent clause — semicolon is wrong. A colon after the complete sentence "The proposal addressed three separate issues" correctly introduces the list. B (comma) is too weak. D (period) is wrong — a fragment would follow.
Example 3 — Complex listHard
The expedition team came from three cities: Cairo, Egypt, Nairobi, Kenya, and Cape Town, South Africa.
  • A. No change
  • B. Cairo, Egypt; Nairobi, Kenya; and Cape Town, South Africa
  • C. Cairo Egypt, Nairobi Kenya, and Cape Town South Africa
  • D. Cairo Egypt; Nairobi Kenya; Cape Town South Africa
B. When list items themselves contain commas (city, country), semicolons separate the items to prevent confusion. B correctly uses commas within each item and semicolons between items. A uses only commas — it's unclear where one item ends and the next begins. C omits the city-country commas. D omits the country commas and the final "and."

Practice — 5 questions
Step 5
Practice 1
The new policy was controversial among staff; however many employees acknowledged it was necessary.
  • A. No change
  • B. staff; however, many
  • C. staff, however, many
  • D. staff however, many
B. "However" as a transitional adverb needs a semicolon before it AND a comma after it: "clause; however, clause." A is missing the comma after "however." C = comma splice. D = no semicolon.
Practice 2
The athlete trained for six months; preparing meticulously for the championship.
  • A. No change
  • B. months: preparing
  • C. months, preparing
  • D. months. Preparing
C. "Preparing meticulously for the championship" is a participial phrase, not an independent clause — semicolon is wrong. A comma correctly follows a main clause before a participial phrase that describes how the athlete trained. D creates a fragment. B (colon) is also incorrect for the same reason as A — neither semicolon nor colon can precede a participial phrase.
Practice 3
Global temperatures have risen by approximately 1.2°C since pre-industrial times; scientists warn that reaching 1.5°C will trigger irreversible changes in key ecosystems.
  • A. No change — the sentence is correct
  • B. times, scientists
  • C. times: scientists
  • D. times, and scientists
A. Both sides are complete sentences with a related but sequential relationship — temperature rise → scientists' warning. Semicolon is correct. B = comma splice. C = colon — the warning doesn't explain the temperature rise, it's a consequence of it; semicolon is more appropriate. D = comma + "and" works but is weaker than the semicolon.
Practice 4
The exhibition will feature works from three artists: Kenji Yamamoto, who works primarily in watercolour, Sofia Reyes, known for her large-scale installations, and Amara Diallo, a ceramicist based in Dakar.
  • A. No change
  • B. Yamamoto, who works primarily in watercolour; Sofia Reyes, known for her large-scale installations; and Amara Diallo
  • C. Yamamoto who works primarily in watercolour; Sofia Reyes known for her large-scale installations; and Amara Diallo
  • D. Yamamoto; Sofia Reyes; and Amara Diallo
B. Each artist entry contains an internal comma (e.g. "Kenji Yamamoto, who works primarily in watercolour") — so semicolons must separate the three list items. B correctly keeps the internal commas and uses semicolons between the three items. C removes the internal commas. D removes the descriptive phrases entirely.
Practice 5 — Hard
The historian argued that the treaty's failure was inevitable; pointing to the absence of enforcement mechanisms as the fatal flaw.
  • A. No change
  • B. inevitable, pointing
  • C. inevitable: pointing
  • D. inevitable. Pointing
B. "Pointing to the absence of enforcement mechanisms as the fatal flaw" is a participial phrase modifying "the historian" — not an independent clause. Semicolon is wrong. A comma correctly attaches the participial phrase to the main clause. D creates a fragment. C (colon) — "pointing to..." is not an elaboration in a list format.

Semicolons — Everything you need
The rule
Complete independent clause on BOTH sides. Test each side alone. Both must stand as complete sentences.
Two uses
1. Join two independent clauses. 2. Separate complex list items that contain internal commas.
Transitional adverbs
Clause; however, clause. ✓ — semicolon before, comma after. Never: clause, however, clause (comma splice).
Never
Before a phrase or clause fragment · Before a participial phrase · Before a relative clause · Combined with FANBOYS (semicolon + but = wrong)
