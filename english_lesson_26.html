Restrictive vs
Non-Restrictive Clauses
"That" restricts — no commas. "Which" adds — commas required. Getting this right fixes both the that/which choice AND the comma questions that come with it.
~1–2 per moduleTarget: 30 secTest: can you remove it without changing the meaning?
⊂
The two clause types
Step 1
Restrictive clause — "that," no commas

A restrictive clause is essential to the meaning of the sentence. It identifies WHICH thing is being talked about. Remove it, and the sentence loses its specificity or becomes incorrect.

"The study that examined sleep deprivation showed alarming results."
→ Remove it: "The study showed alarming results." — Now we don't know WHICH study. The clause is essential — no commas, use "that."

"The students that submitted their work on time received full credit."
→ Identifies which students. Essential. No commas.

Non-restrictive clause — "which," commas required

A non-restrictive clause is extra information — a parenthetical that adds detail but doesn't change which thing is being discussed. Remove it and the sentence still means the same thing.

"The Amazon River, which flows through nine countries, carries more water than any other river."
→ Remove it: "The Amazon River carries more water than any other river." — Still identifies the same river. The clause is extra — commas required, use "which."

"Her first novel, which was written in six weeks, became a bestseller."
→ We already know which novel (her first). The detail is extra. Commas + "which."

The removal test

Remove the clause. Does the sentence still clearly refer to the same thing?
If YES → non-restrictive → "which" + commas
If NO (meaning changes or becomes vague) → restrictive → "that" + no commas

⭐
Strategy
Step 2
1
Find the relative clause — the part beginning with "that" or "which" (or "who" for people).
2
Apply the removal test. Cross out the clause. Does the sentence still clearly identify the same noun? Yes → non-restrictive (which + commas). No → restrictive (that + no commas).
3
Check the commas match the choice of word. "That" → no commas before it. "Which" → comma before it (and after it if mid-sentence). Never "that" with commas or "which" without commas.
⚠️
Traps
Step 3
Trap 1
"Which" without commas
"The policy which was introduced last year reduced emissions." "Which" requires commas: "The policy, which was introduced last year, reduced emissions." Or switch to "that" if the clause is restrictive.
Trap 2
"That" with commas
"The study, that found the link, was published in Nature." Wrong — "that" never takes commas. Either remove the commas (restrictive) or change to "which" (non-restrictive with commas).
Trap 3
Only one comma of a pair
"The report, which took three years to complete was widely praised." The closing comma after "complete" is missing. Non-restrictive clauses mid-sentence need commas on BOTH sides.
Trap 4
"Who" for people
"That" vs "which" applies to things. For people, use "who" (restrictive or non-restrictive) — not "that" when referring to a person. "The scientist who discovered penicillin" not "the scientist that."

Worked examples
Step 4
Example 1 — Removal testMedium
The legislation, that was passed in 2019, significantly expanded access to affordable housing in urban areas.
  • A. No change
  • B. which
  • C. Remove the commas and keep "that"
  • D. and it
B. Removal test: "The legislation significantly expanded access..." — still clearly refers to the same legislation. The clause is extra information → non-restrictive → "which" + commas. A = "that" with commas — never correct. C = removes commas, keeps "that" — the clause would then be restrictive, but the commas were there for a reason; the real fix is "which."
Example 2 — Restrictive: which → thatMedium
Researchers should focus on the trials which produced statistically significant results rather than those with inconclusive findings.
  • A. No change
  • B. that
  • C. , which
  • D. , that
B. Removal test: "Researchers should focus on the trials rather than those..." — "the trials" is now vague. The clause tells us WHICH trials (those with significant results). Essential → restrictive → "that" with no commas. A ("which" without commas) violates the "which needs commas" rule. C (comma + which) would make it non-restrictive — but removing the clause loses the essential meaning. D (comma + that) = never correct.
Example 3 — Missing closing commaHard
The glacier, which has retreated by forty metres per year since the 1980s now covers less than half the area it did a century ago.
  • A. No change
  • B. 1980s, now covers
  • C. 1980s — now covers
  • D. 1980s. It now covers
B. The non-restrictive clause "which has retreated by forty metres per year since the 1980s" is opened with a comma after "glacier" — it must also be closed with a comma. The closing comma is missing after "1980s." B adds it. C (dash) — mixes punctuation styles (comma opens, dash closes = wrong pairing). D splits into two sentences — loses the non-restrictive structure.

Practice — 5 questions
Step 5
Practice 1
The drug which targets the specific protein responsible for cell proliferation has shown remarkable efficacy in early trials.
  • A. No change
  • B. that
  • C. , which
  • D. , that
B. Removal test: "The drug has shown remarkable efficacy..." — we don't know which drug. The clause is essential — restrictive → "that" + no commas. A ("which" without commas) violates the rule. C (comma + which) = non-restrictive — but removing the clause loses the essential identifier. D (comma + that) = never correct.
Practice 2
The Eiffel Tower, that was completed in 1889 as a temporary exhibit for the World's Fair, remains one of the most visited monuments in the world.
  • A. No change
  • B. which
  • C. Remove the commas and keep "that"
  • D. and it
B. "The Eiffel Tower" is already uniquely identified — we know exactly which structure. Removal test: "The Eiffel Tower remains one of the most visited monuments" — still perfectly clear. Non-restrictive → "which" + commas (both already present). A = "that" + commas = never correct.
Practice 3
Students who complete the optional reading assignments tend to perform significantly better on assessments than those who rely solely on lecture notes.
  • A. No change — the sentence is correctly punctuated
  • B. Students, who complete the optional reading assignments,
  • C. Students that complete the optional reading assignments
  • D. Students, that complete the optional reading assignments,
A. "Who complete the optional reading assignments" identifies WHICH students — the ones who do optional reading. Essential → restrictive → "who" with no commas. Correct as written. B adds commas — makes it non-restrictive, which changes the meaning to "all students complete optional reading." C uses "that" for people — acceptable but "who" is preferred and A is already correct. D = "that" + commas = never correct.
Practice 4
The committee approved the budget proposal, which allocated $4.2 million to infrastructure upgrades, but rejected the staffing plan outright.
  • A. No change — the sentence is correct
  • B. proposal that allocated $4.2 million to infrastructure upgrades
  • C. proposal, that allocated $4.2 million to infrastructure upgrades,
  • D. proposal which allocated $4.2 million to infrastructure upgrades
A. "The budget proposal" is already specific — we know which proposal. Removal test: "The committee approved the budget proposal, but rejected the staffing plan" — still perfectly clear. Non-restrictive → "which" + commas (both present). B makes it restrictive — loses the essential non-restrictive meaning. C = "that" + commas = wrong. D = "which" without commas = wrong.
Practice 5 — Hard
The city has invested in a new tram network, which connects the airport to the city centre has already reduced private car usage by 18%.
  • A. No change
  • B. which connects the airport to the city centre,
  • C. that connects the airport to the city centre,
  • D. connecting the airport to the city centre,
B. "A new tram network" is already identified. The clause "which connects the airport to the city centre" adds extra detail — non-restrictive. It needs commas on BOTH sides: one after "network" (already there) and one after "centre" (missing in A). B adds the closing comma. C = "that" with closing comma — "that" never takes commas. D = participle — changes the grammatical structure of the clause.

Summary card
Keep this
Restrictive vs Non-Restrictive — Everything you need
Restrictive ("that")
Essential to meaning. Identifies WHICH thing. Remove it → meaning changes or becomes vague. Use "that." No commas. Ever.
Non-restrictive ("which")
Extra information. The noun is already identified. Remove it → sentence still means the same thing. Use "which." Commas on BOTH sides.
The removal test
Cross out the clause. Still clear? → non-restrictive (which + commas). Now vague? → restrictive (that + no commas).
The four never-dos
Never "that" + commas · Never "which" without commas · Never only one comma of a pair · Never "that" for people (use "who")
People → "who"
"The scientist who discovered X" not "the scientist that." "Who" works for both restrictive and non-restrictive when referring to people.
Paired commas rule
Non-restrictive clause mid-sentence ALWAYS needs commas on both sides. Missing the closing comma is a very common SAT error.
