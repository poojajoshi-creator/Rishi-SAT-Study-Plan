Words in Context
~4–5 questions · 45 sec each
The only strategy that works
Cover the choices. Read the sentence. Supply your own word first. Then match your word to the options. The SAT never uses the most common meaning of the underlined word. Your substitution bypasses every trap.
Core rule
Context overrides everything
The correct meaning is entirely determined by how the word is used in this specific sentence. "Sanction" means approve or punish. "Table" means postpone. "Volatile" means unpredictable in finance, evaporates easily in chemistry. Ignore your instincts about common usage.
Picking the most familiar or common meaning of the word.
Wrong answer pattern
Valid synonym, wrong context
Trap answers are real synonyms of the word — just not in this context. They sound right because they're genuinely related. Your substituted word (before looking at choices) is the only defence against this.
Picking an answer that's a valid synonym in general but wrong for this sentence.

Text Structure & Purpose
~3–4 questions · 60 sec each
The WHY-not-WHAT rule
Correct answers describe WHY a sentence exists (its function) — not what it says. Ask: "What would the passage lose if this sentence were deleted?" That function is the answer. Eliminate every choice that summarises content instead of describing purpose.
7 sentence functions
Name it before you answer
Introduce — sets up a topic or question
Support/Illustrate — provides evidence or a concrete example
Complicate — adds nuance or a caveat to the previous claim
Contrast — sets up an opposing view
Conclude — draws examples into a pattern
Transition — bridges two sections
Qualify — limits the scope of the claim
Add / Delete / Revise
The 2-question test
1. What is this paragraph's main point? State it in one sentence.

2. Does this sentence serve that specific point?

If No → delete or don't add. "Interesting and factually accurate" ≠ "serves this paragraph."
Keeping a sentence because it's related to the broad topic but not this paragraph's specific point.

Cross-Text Connections
~3–4 questions · 90 sec each
Do this before every cross-text question — non-negotiable
After reading both passages, write or say: "Author 1 argues ___. Author 2 argues ___." Then name the relationship between those two sentences. Only then read the question and choices.
5 relationship types
Name it precisely
Agreement — same conclusion, possibly different evidence
Contradiction — one says X is true, other says X is false
Complication — "Yes, but…" — adds caveat to Passage 1
Extension — builds further in the same direction
Methodological critique — attacks the evidence, not the conclusion

Calibrate strength: "challenges" ≠ "completely refutes." Avoid answers that are too strong or too weak.
3 question forms
Different approaches
Point-of-view — use only evidence in the passage. Never infer beyond what's stated.

"Both agree" — find the overlap. Usually more general than either author's specific claim. Eliminate anything one passage contradicts.

Hypothetical evidence — apply the evidence to each passage separately, then pick the answer that correctly describes the effect on both.
Answers that say "completely refutes," "fully proves," or "definitively establishes" — almost always too strong.

Expression of Ideas
~8–12 questions per module
Transitions — classify first, always
6 types
Contrast: however, nevertheless, yet, whereas, in contrast
Cause/Result: therefore, thus, consequently, as a result
Addition: furthermore, additionally, moreover
Example: for example, for instance, specifically
Concession: although, even though, while, admittedly
Restatement: in other words, that is, namely

Concession ≠ Contrast. Concession = "yes, but…" Contrast = "this, not that."
Picking what sounds right instead of classifying the relationship first.
Rhetorical Synthesis — goal first
3-step method
Step 1: Read and underline the goal. The verb matters: "emphasize," "contrast," "explain the cause," "introduce to an unfamiliar reader."

Step 2: Eliminate any answer that doesn't accomplish that exact goal — even if it uses accurate facts from the notes.

Step 3: Among survivors, pick the most complete and direct one.
Picking the answer with the most facts instead of the one that accomplishes the specific goal.

Standard English Conventions
~11–15 questions per module
3-step SEC method — every question
1. Identify what's being tested — look at how the four choices differ (punctuation only? verb form? pronoun? modifier?). 2. Apply the rule directly — do NOT trust your ear. 3. Verify — read the full sentence with your answer to confirm.
RuleWhat to know
Sentence boundariesTwo independent clauses need: semicolon / comma + FANBOYS / period. A comma alone = comma splice (wrong). Nothing = run-on (wrong).
Subject-verb agreementCross out all words between subject and verb. Collective nouns (team, committee, board) = singular. Neither/nor: agree with the closer subject.
Pronoun agreementCollective nouns → "it." Ambiguous "they" = wrong. Pronoun must clearly refer to exactly one antecedent.
Colon vs semicolonColon: complete sentence before it, introduces a list or explanation. Semicolon: complete sentence on BOTH sides (= period in disguise).
Paired dashesTwo dashes = parenthetical (like two commas). If a closing dash already exists, the opening must also be a dash — not a comma or colon.
Verb tense"By the time [past event]" → past perfect (had + verb). "Since" → present perfect. Keep tense consistent unless the timeline requires a shift.
Dangling modifierOpening participial phrase → subject immediately after the comma MUST be what the phrase describes. "Having studied, the exam felt easy" = WRONG (the exam didn't study).
ParallelismAll items in a series must use the same grammatical form. "She likes swimming, running, and to cycle" = WRONG. All gerunds or all infinitives.

In-test quick reminders
When stuck — eliminate
4 elimination rules
Cross out any answer that:
• Uses "always," "never," "proves," "completely" — usually too strong
• Describes WHAT instead of WHY (text structure)
• Doesn't accomplish the stated goal (synthesis)
• Agrees with the passage that was supposed to challenge

Eliminating two wrong answers doubles your odds on the remaining two.

Algebra
~35% of Math
Linear equations & systems
Key patterns
y = mx + b — slope m, intercept b
Parallel lines = same slope, different intercept → 0 solutions
Same line = same slope AND intercept → ∞ solutions
Perpendicular slope = negative reciprocal of original

For a₁x+b₁y=c₁ and a₂x+b₂y=c₂:
a₁/a₂ = b₁/b₂ ≠ c₁/c₂ → no solution
a₁/a₂ = b₁/b₂ = c₁/c₂ → infinite solutions
Inequalities & absolute value
Rules
Flip the sign when multiplying/dividing by a negative.

|ax + b| = c creates TWO equations:
ax + b = c   AND   ax + b = −c
Solve both.

|x| < a → −a < x < a
|x| > a → x < −a OR x > a
Forgetting to flip the inequality sign when dividing by a negative.
Functions
Transformations & inverses
f(x) + k → shifts UP k
f(x + h) → shifts LEFT h  (trap: not right)
−f(x) → reflects over x-axis
f(−x) → reflects over y-axis
a·f(x) → vertical stretch by a

Composite f(g(x)): apply g first, then f
Inverse f⁻¹: swap x and y, solve for y
f(x+h) shifts LEFT, not right — because you need x+h=0 so x=−h.
Word problems
Setup rules
Define variables explicitly before writing equations.

Rate × Time = Distance/Work
Simple interest: I = Prt
Percent: Part = (% ÷ 100) × Whole
% change: (new − old) / old × 100

Always ask: does my answer make sense in the real-world context?

Advanced Math
~35% of Math
Quadratics — all three forms
Know when to use each
Standard: ax²+bx+c → factorise or use formula
Vertex: a(x−h)²+k → vertex = (h,k), axis = x=h
Factored: a(x−r₁)(x−r₂) → roots at r₁, r₂

Quadratic formula: x = (−b ± √(b²−4ac)) / 2a
Discriminant: >0 = two real roots, =0 = one, <0 = none
Sum of roots = −b/a · Product of roots = c/a
Polynomials & rational functions
Key techniques
Factor theorem: if p(a) = 0, then (x−a) is a factor
Difference of squares: a²−b² = (a+b)(a−b)
Perfect square: a²±2ab+b² = (a±b)²

Cancelling factors: (x²−4)/(x−2) = x+2 only for x≠2. The restriction on the domain survives simplification — it's a hole, not an asymptote.
Cancelling (x−2) and forgetting x=2 is still excluded from the domain.
Exponents & radicals
Rules cold
aᵐ×aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ  ·  aᵐ/aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ  ·  (aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ
a⁰ = 1  ·  a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ

Fractional exponent: a^(m/n) = (ⁿ√a)ᵐ
Take the root first, then raise to the power.
27^(2/3) = (∛27)² = 3² = 9
Exponential models
Growth, decay, half-life
General: y = a · bˣ    b = 1+r (growth) or 1−r (decay)
Half-life: y = a · (½)^(t/h)
Doubling every d units: exponent is t/d, not t
Compound interest: A = P(1 + r/n)^(nt)

The exponent structure controls the rate. 2^(t/3) doubles every 3 units.
Writing 2^t when the quantity doubles every 3 units — the correct exponent is t/3.

Problem-Solving & Data Analysis
~15% of Math
Statistics
Always-tested concepts
Mean = sum / count  ·  Median = middle value
SD measures spread, not centre

Adding constant to all values → mean shifts, SD unchanged
Multiplying all values by k → both mean and SD multiply by k

IQR = Q3 − Q1
Outlier if > Q3 + 1.5×IQR or < Q1 − 1.5×IQR
Adding a constant changes the SD. It does NOT — only spread changes on multiplication.
Correlation & causation
Most-tested data concept
Correlation ≠ causation.
Observational study (survey, scatter plot) → association only, never cause.

Causation requires a controlled experiment with random assignment.

Lurking variables may explain both variables.

Margin of error ∝ 1/√n. To halve MOE, quadruple sample size.
Concluding causation from a strong correlation (r = 0.95 does NOT prove causation).
Probability
With vs without replacement
Independent: P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B)
Or: P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A and B)

Without replacement: denominator decreases after each draw.
5 red of 10 → P(1st red) = 5/10, P(2nd red | 1st red) = 4/9
Using the same denominator for both draws when drawing WITHOUT replacement.
Ratios & unit conversion
Unit analysis
Set up conversions as fractions that cancel:
60 mi/hr × 5280 ft/mi × 1 hr/3600 s = 88 ft/s

Weighted average = Σ(value × weight) / Σ(weights)

Ratios a:b:c = 3:5:2 with total 500:
Each part = 500/10 = 50 → a=150, b=250, c=100

Geometry & Trigonometry
~15% of Math
Triangles
Must know cold
Pythagorean theorem: a²+b²=c²
Triples: 3-4-5 · 5-12-13 · 8-15-17 · 7-24-25
30-60-90: x, x√3, 2x
45-45-90: x, x, x√2
Similar triangles: sides proportional; area ratio = (side ratio)²
Exterior angle = sum of two non-adjacent interior angles
Larger angle → longer opposite side
Circles
All formulas
Area: πr²  ·  Circumference: 2πr
Arc length: (θ/360) × 2πr
Sector area: (θ/360) × πr²
Equation: (x−h)²+(y−k)²=r², centre=(h,k)

From general form: complete the square on x and y terms, adding the same values to both sides.
Using the diameter as the radius in area or sector calculations.
Trigonometry
SOH-CAH-TOA + identities
sin=opp/hyp  ·  cos=adj/hyp  ·  tan=opp/adj

Identity: sin²θ + cos²θ = 1
Co-function: sin(90°−θ) = cosθ

Quadrant signs:
QI: all +  ·  QII: sin+ cos− tan−
QIII: sin− cos− tan+  ·  QIV: sin− cos+ tan−

sin(180°−θ) = sinθ
Ignoring the quadrant — sign matters for tan and cos especially.
Volume & 3D
Key formulas
Cylinder: πr²h
Cone: ⅓πr²h
Sphere: ⁴⁄₃πr³  ·  SA = 4πr²
Box: l × w × h

Scale factor k: lengths ×k, areas ×k², volumes ×k³

Frustum = full cone − small cone. Small cone radius scales by same ratio as height.

Calculator & pacing tips
Calculator module
Use it strategically
The calculator module has harder questions, not just bigger numbers. Don't rely on the calculator for thinking — use it to verify.

Plug-in strategy: for "which value satisfies this equation" — try the answer choices directly.

Graph it: for quadratic/function questions, graphing often reveals the answer immediately.
No-calculator module
Accuracy first
Write every arithmetic step — never mental multi-step calculations.

If numbers look ugly, you're probably overcomplicating — look for a factoring shortcut or pattern.

Grid-in questions: if your answer is negative or unreasonably large, recheck your setup.

After the test — error log
The most important 30 minutes of the week
Wait 30 minutes after finishing before reviewing. Then for every wrong answer, record: (1) question type, (2) what you chose and why, (3) correct answer, (4) which trap you fell into. This error log is what Days 7 and 8 are built on.
English errors — log by type
☐ Words in Context — wrong substitution?
☐ Text Structure — described content not function?
☐ Cross-Text — relationship wrong? Strength wrong?
☐ Transitions — picked without classifying first?
☐ Synthesis — picked facts over goal?
☐ Add/Delete — kept irrelevant sentence?
☐ SEC — which rule? (punctuation / agreement / modifier / tense?)
Math errors — log by error type
☐ Concept error — didn't know the rule
☐ Setup error — right method, wrong equation
☐ Arithmetic error — right method, calculation mistake
☐ Trap — chose the answer the SAT wanted you to pick
☐ Time error — rushed, made avoidable mistakes
☐ Reading error — misread the question or diagram

Error TYPE matters as much as knowing the right answer.
