The SAT never tests what a word usually means. It tests what the word means in this exact sentence. That distinction is everything.
~4–5 questions per module Target: 45 sec per question Strategy: cover → supply → match
📖
What is Words in Context?
Step 1
Core definition

A Words in Context question gives you a passage with one word or phrase underlined. Your job is to choose the answer that best captures what that word means as it is used in this specific passage.

This sounds simple. It isn't. The SAT deliberately selects words that have multiple valid meanings — and the most familiar meaning is almost never the right answer. The question is testing whether you can read context, not whether you know definitions.

What the question is really asking: "If you replaced the underlined word with your answer, would the sentence mean exactly the same thing in exactly this context?"

Why it's harder than it looks

Every word in English carries a primary meaning (the first one in the dictionary) and several secondary or specialised meanings. The SAT almost always uses a secondary meaning — and one of the wrong answer choices will be the primary meaning.

Examples of words the SAT loves:

• "Table" — most common meaning: a piece of furniture. SAT meaning: to postpone or set aside (as in "table this discussion")
• "Sanction" — most common: a penalty or restriction. SAT meaning: to officially approve or permit
• "Qualify" — most common: to meet requirements. SAT meaning: to limit or add conditions to a statement
• "Check" — most common: to verify. SAT meaning: to restrain or hold back
• "Precipitate" — most common: rain or snow. SAT meaning: to cause something to happen suddenly

🎯
How it appears on the SAT
Step 2
Typical question stems

The question will say one of these things — they all mean the same thing:

• "As used in the text, what does the word ___ most nearly mean?"
• "As used in line X, ___ most nearly means..."
• "The word ___ as used in the passage most nearly means..."

The phrase "most nearly means" is key — it tells you they want the closest equivalent in this context, not a dictionary definition.

What the passage looks like

The passage is usually 2–6 sentences long. One word is underlined (sometimes bold or in quotation marks). You get four answer choices — all of which are real synonyms of the word, but only one fits this exact context.

The passage always gives you enough context to determine the correct meaning. The answer is always provable from the text — you should be able to point to specific words in the passage that confirm your choice.

⭐
The strategy — Cover · Supply · Match
Step 3

This three-step method works on every Words in Context question, every time. Learn it until it's automatic.

1
Cover the answer choices with your hand or a finger. Do not look at them yet. Looking at the choices first plants their words in your brain and makes the trap answers feel right.
2
Read the sentence (or two sentences) around the underlined word. Ask yourself: "What meaning would make this sentence true?" Supply your own word — even a rough one. Write it in the margin if you can. This is the most important step.
3
Now look at the choices. Find the one that closest matches YOUR word. Don't change your mind because a choice "sounds smarter" — trust the word you supplied from context.
✓
Verify: substitute your answer back into the sentence. Read it aloud in your head. Does the sentence still mean exactly the same thing? If yes, you're done. If it feels slightly off, the next closest choice may be better.
Why Step 1 matters so much

The SAT designs its wrong answer choices to be real synonyms of the underlined word. If you read the choices before forming your own idea, you'll be choosing between synonyms — and all four will seem plausible.

If you supply your own word first, you have a specific target. Then you're just matching your target to the closest option. That's much easier than choosing between four plausible-sounding synonyms.

⚠️
The four traps
Step 4
Trap 1
The primary meaning
The most common or familiar meaning of the word is always one of the wrong choices. Students pick it because it's what they know.
"The discovery would table the debate" — wrong choice: a piece of furniture. Correct: to postpone.
Trap 2
True but wrong context
A word that is a genuine synonym of the underlined word but doesn't fit this specific context. All four choices are real synonyms — only one fits here.
"Volatile" in finance means unpredictable. In chemistry it means evaporates easily. Only one fits the passage.
Trap 3
The "sounds sophisticated" trap
One wrong choice is always a more formal or technical-sounding word that feels like it elevates the passage. Students pick it to sound smart.
Correct answer: "limit." Trap answer: "circumscribe." Circumscribe IS a real synonym — but if the context says "limit," use limit.
Trap 4
Reading too far
Students read the whole passage and choose a word that fits the overall theme rather than the specific sentence. Always zoom in on the exact sentence.
A passage about ambition and drive. Underlined word: "pressing." Correct: urgent. Wrong trap: ambitious (fits the theme but not this sentence).

Worked examples — with full thinking
Step 5

For each example, notice how the Cover → Supply → Match method is applied before looking at choices.

Example 1 — Common secondary meaning Medium
The committee decided to table the proposed amendment until further research could be conducted on its potential economic consequences.
💭 Step-by-step thinking
Step 1: Cover the choices.
Step 2: Read the sentence. "Decided to ___ the amendment until further research." The word "until" is crucial — something is being delayed. My word: postpone or delay.
Step 3: Now look at choices.
As used in the text, "table" most nearly means:
  • A. place on a flat surface
  • B. organise into a chart
  • C. postpone for a later time
  • D. present formally for discussion
Answer: C — postpone for a later time.

My word was "postpone" — C matches exactly. The phrase "until further research could be conducted" confirms something is being delayed, not presented or filed.

Why A is wrong: "place on a surface" is the most familiar meaning of "table" — this is Trap 1, the primary meaning trap.
Why D is wrong: "present for discussion" is actually the UK/parliamentary meaning of "table," which is the opposite of the US meaning used here. This is a subtle trap.
Why B is wrong: "organise into a chart" = tabling as in making a table — wrong domain entirely.
Example 2 — Context changes everything Medium
Far from weakening under criticism, the researcher's confidence in her methodology only hardened when peers challenged her results — she had anticipated their objections and had prepared responses to each one.
💭 Step-by-step thinking
Step 2: Read carefully. "Confidence... only ___ when challenged." The word "only" signals that the expected thing (weakening) did NOT happen — instead something stronger occurred. My word: grew stronger or intensified.
Do NOT pick a meaning related to physical hardness.
As used in the text, "hardened" most nearly means:
  • A. became physically rigid or solid
  • B. became stronger and more resolute
  • C. grew callous or unfeeling
  • D. became accustomed through exposure
Answer: B — became stronger and more resolute.

My word was "grew stronger" — B matches. The context shows confidence increasing under pressure, not physical change, emotional numbness, or habituation.

Why A is wrong: physically rigid — the most literal meaning. Trap 1.
Why C is wrong: "callous" fits contexts like "hardened criminals" — emotionally unfeeling. But the passage is about confidence, not emotion. Wrong context (Trap 2).
Why D is wrong: "hardened to" something means became accustomed — but "hardened" alone in this sentence means intensified.
Example 3 — Two-sentence context Hard
The new policy was intended to check the rising tide of urban development that had consumed green spaces at an alarming rate. Without intervention, planners warned, the remaining parks and woodland corridors would be lost within a decade.
💭 Step-by-step thinking
Step 2: Read both sentences. "Policy intended to ___ the rising tide." A tide that "consumed green spaces at an alarming rate" needs to be stopped, slowed, or controlled. My word: stop or slow down or restrain.
Notice: "check" here is not about verifying — that's the primary meaning trap.
As used in the text, "check" most nearly means:
  • A. verify or confirm
  • B. mark with a tick or symbol
  • C. restrain or hold back
  • D. examine carefully for errors
Answer: C — restrain or hold back.

My word was "restrain" — C matches exactly. The context makes clear something is growing that needs to be controlled.

Why A and D are wrong: both relate to "verify/examine" — the most common meaning of "check." Classic Trap 1.
Why B is wrong: "mark with a tick" is another primary meaning — wrong domain entirely.
Example 4 — Positive/negative shift Hard
While some economists qualify their support for the new tariff regime, arguing that its benefits depend heavily on trading partners' responses, others have endorsed it without reservation.
💭 Step-by-step thinking
Step 2: "Economists ___ their support, arguing that benefits depend on..." The economists do support it — but with conditions. They're saying "yes, but..." My word: limit or add conditions to.
"Qualify" here does NOT mean "to become eligible for."
As used in the text, "qualify" most nearly means:
  • A. become eligible or certified
  • B. demonstrate competence
  • C. describe in detail
  • D. limit or add conditions to
Answer: D — limit or add conditions to.

My word was "add conditions to" — D matches. The economists support the tariff BUT with conditions ("depends heavily on..."). This is the academic/grammatical meaning of "qualify" — to modify or restrict a statement.

Why A is wrong: "become eligible" — the most common meaning. Trap 1.
Why B is wrong: "demonstrate competence" — another primary meaning. Trap 1.
Why C is wrong: "describe in detail" — sounds plausible but the context shows restriction, not description.

Practice — 5 questions
Step 6

Apply the Cover → Supply → Match method. Do not look at choices until you have your own word.

Practice 1
The senator's speech was seen as an attempt to court suburban voters whose support would be critical in the upcoming election.
As used in the text, "court" most nearly means:
  • A. a paved outdoor area
  • B. a legal tribunal
  • C. seek to win the favour of
  • D. challenge in competition
C — seek to win the favour of. The senator is trying to win voters over. "Court" here means to actively pursue someone's support. A (outdoor area) and B (tribunal) are the primary meanings — Trap 1.
Practice 2
The author's argument, while compelling on its surface, rested on several assumptions that the reviewer found difficult to verify.
As used in the text, "compelling" most nearly means:
  • A. forcibly demanding compliance
  • B. persuasive and convincing
  • C. emotionally distressing
  • D. legally obligatory
B — persuasive and convincing. The reviewer found the argument convincing on the surface but had doubts about its foundations. A ("forcibly demanding") is the literal root meaning. D (legally obligatory) is a technical meaning. C doesn't fit — arguments aren't distressing.
Practice 3
The museum's collection, once celebrated for its breadth, had suffered decades of neglect and now represented only a fraction of its former scope.
As used in the text, "celebrated" most nearly means:
  • A. given a party or ceremony in honour of
  • B. considered famous for an achievement
  • C. widely admired and praised
  • D. officially commemorated
C — widely admired and praised. The collection was well-regarded for its breadth. B is close but "famous for an achievement" adds specificity not in the text. C is cleaner — the collection was admired. A and D are the primary meanings (holding celebrations).
Practice 4
Scientists have long sought to isolate the specific gene responsible for the condition, but the trait appears to be influenced by dozens of genetic variants working in combination.
As used in the text, "isolate" most nearly means:
  • A. place in quarantine
  • B. make lonely or separated from society
  • C. identify and extract as a single distinct factor
  • D. remove from a dangerous environment
C — identify and extract as a single distinct factor. The scientists want to find the ONE gene responsible. "Isolate" in a scientific context means to separate out a single element for study. The contrast ("but... dozens of variants working in combination") confirms they're seeking a single cause.
Practice 5 — Hard
The report's conclusions were measured — the authors deliberately avoided sweeping claims, instead offering carefully limited assessments tied to specific conditions and caveats.
As used in the text, "measured" most nearly means:
  • A. calculated using instruments or units
  • B. rhythmic and evenly paced
  • C. restrained and carefully considered
  • D. quantifiably precise
C — restrained and carefully considered. The passage says the authors "avoided sweeping claims" and offered "carefully limited assessments with caveats." This defines measured as cautious/restrained. A and D are the primary meanings (measuring with instruments). B ("rhythmic") applies to measured prose or music — wrong domain.

Words in Context — Everything you need
The method
Cover → Supply → Match
Cover choices. Read the sentence. Supply YOUR word. Then match to choices. Never look at choices first.
The core rule
The SAT always uses a secondary or unusual meaning. The most common meaning of the word is almost always a wrong answer.
Trap 1
Primary meaning — most familiar definition of the word. Always present as a wrong choice.
Trap 2
True synonym, wrong context — a real alternative meaning that doesn't fit this specific sentence.
Trap 3
Sophisticated-sounding — a formal word that feels elevated. Pick the word that fits, not the one that sounds impressive.
Trap 4
Fits the theme — matches the passage topic but not the specific sentence. Zoom in on the sentence, not the whole passage.
Verification step
Substitute your answer back into the sentence. If the sentence means exactly the same thing, you're correct.
Time target
45 seconds per question. If you supplied your word before looking at choices, this is very achievable.
