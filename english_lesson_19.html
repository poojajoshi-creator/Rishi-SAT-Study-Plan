Concept 19 / 27SEC — Punctuation
Dashes
Dashes are the most flexible punctuation mark — they can do what commas, colons, and parentheses do, but with more emphasis. The key rule: paired dashes always come in pairs.
~1–2 per moduleTarget: 30 secRule: if one dash opens, another must close
—
How dashes work
Step 1
Two uses of the dash

Use 1 — Single dash: dramatic pause or elaboration
A single dash introduces an abrupt elaboration, explanation, or emphasis at the end of a sentence — like a colon but more dramatic.
"She made one mistake that cost her everything — she trusted the wrong person."
"The study confirmed what researchers had long suspected — stress accelerates cellular ageing."

Use 2 — Paired dashes: parenthetical interruption
Two dashes frame parenthetical information — an aside, definition, or example — that could be removed without breaking the sentence. They function like two commas but with greater emphasis.
"The discovery — made entirely by accident — transformed the field."
"Her first novel — written in six weeks during a bout of insomnia — became an international bestseller."

Dash vs comma vs parentheses

All three can frame a parenthetical, but with different emphasis:
Lowest: (parentheses) — quietest, most dismissible
Middle: comma, comma — standard
Highest: dash — dash — loudest, most emphatic
The SAT tests whether you use a dash where a comma belongs or vice versa — and especially whether you close a paired dash.

⭐
Strategy
Step 2
1
Look for the opening dash. If you see a dash opening a parenthetical, the closing punctuation MUST also be a dash. Cannot close with a comma if opened with a dash.
2
Test the parenthetical by removing it. Read the sentence without the dashed phrase. Does it still make complete sense? If yes — the dashes are correctly framing a parenthetical.
3
Single dash at end: check it introduces an elaboration. What follows the dash should explain or dramatically restate what came before — not introduce a new unrelated idea.
⚠️
Traps
Step 3
Trap 1 — most common
Mixed pairs: dash + comma
"The results — which surprised everyone, were published immediately." The opening is a dash but the closing is a comma. Wrong. Both must match: either two dashes or two commas.
Trap 2
Dash where colon is needed
Both dash and colon can introduce elaboration, but a colon needs a complete sentence before it. A dash is more flexible. The SAT usually accepts both — check whether the choices offer both and pick the one that matches the surrounding style.
Trap 3
Three dashes in one sentence
A sentence can't have three dashes — one opens the parenthetical, one closes it, and a third would create confusion about what's paired with what. Restructure if you see three.

Worked examples
Step 4
Example 1 — Mixed pair errorMedium
The engineer's solution — elegant in its simplicity, reduced production costs by a third without sacrificing quality.
  • A. No change
  • B. solution — elegant in its simplicity — reduced
  • C. solution, elegant in its simplicity, reduced
  • D. solution: elegant in its simplicity, reduced
B. The opening dash after "solution" must be matched with a closing dash. "Elegant in its simplicity" is parenthetical — remove it and "The engineer's solution reduced production costs" makes sense. A mixes dash + comma — wrong pairing. C uses two commas — acceptable but A already has the dash opening, so B is the fix. D (colon) — colons don't close parenthetical insertions mid-sentence.
Example 2 — Single dash for elaborationEasy
The committee reached a decision that no one had anticipated _______ they voted to dissolve themselves entirely.
  • A. anticipated, they
  • B. anticipated — they
  • C. anticipated; they
  • D. anticipated: they
B. The dash introduces a dramatic revelation — what the unexpected decision WAS. This is the single-dash elaboration use. A = comma splice. C (semicolon) works structurally but lacks the dramatic emphasis that the context ("no one had anticipated") calls for. D (colon) also works, but the dash is more dramatic and appropriate for this context.
Example 3 — Choosing between comma and dashHard
The prime minister's speech, delivered to a packed chamber — outlined a radical departure from forty years of economic policy.
  • A. No change
  • B. speech, delivered to a packed chamber, outlined
  • C. speech — delivered to a packed chamber — outlined
  • D. speech: delivered to a packed chamber, outlined
B. "Delivered to a packed chamber" is a parenthetical — it needs matching punctuation on both sides. The original opens with a comma but closes with a dash — mixed pair, wrong. B uses comma + comma (consistent). C uses dash + dash (also correct). But the context doesn't call for dramatic emphasis — B is the cleaner choice. D opens with a colon (wrong — colons don't open mid-sentence parentheticals) and closes with a comma.

Practice — 5 questions
Step 5
Practice 1
The physicist's breakthrough — described by colleagues as the most significant advance in the field in fifty years, earned her the Nobel Prize in Physics.
  • A. No change
  • B. years — earned
  • C. years, earned
  • D. years: earned
B. The sentence opens the parenthetical with a dash ("breakthrough —"). The closing punctuation must also be a dash. B closes with " — earned." A closes with a comma — mixed pair. C changes the closing to a comma — still mixed. D uses a colon — wrong for closing a parenthetical.
Practice 2
Only one thing could have saved the project at that point — a complete redesign of the underlying architecture.
  • A. No change — the sentence is correctly punctuated
  • B. point, a complete redesign
  • C. point: a complete redesign
  • D. point; a complete redesign
A. Single dash correctly introduces the dramatic revelation of what "only one thing" was. The elaboration ("a complete redesign of the underlying architecture") explains "only one thing." B (comma) is too weak for this emphatic context. C (colon) also works structurally — both colon and single dash are valid here, but A is correct as given. D (semicolon) — "a complete redesign" is not an independent clause.
Practice 3
The report — compiled over three years by a team of sixteen researchers, represents the most comprehensive analysis of urban housing affordability ever conducted in this country.
  • A. No change
  • B. researchers — represents
  • C. researchers, represents
  • D. researchers: represents
B. Opening dash → must close with dash. "Compiled over three years by a team of sixteen researchers" is parenthetical. Remove it: "The report represents the most comprehensive analysis..." — complete. The closing must be a dash. B provides the matching closing dash.
Practice 4
She had one rule she never broke — always read the contract before signing.
  • A. No change
  • B. broke, always
  • C. broke; always
  • D. broke: always
A. Single dash introduces what "the one rule" was — emphatic elaboration. "Always read the contract before signing" IS the rule. The dash is appropriate for its dramatic reveal function. D (colon) also works — but A is correct as given. B = comma is too weak. C = semicolon — "always read..." is not an independent clause.
Practice 5 — Hard
The architect's design — which incorporated recycled materials and passive solar heating — and her innovative financing model, together made the project viable.
  • A. No change — the sentence is correctly punctuated
  • B. design, which incorporated recycled materials and passive solar heating, and her innovative financing model, together
  • C. design — which incorporated recycled materials and passive solar heating, and her innovative financing model — together
  • D. design which incorporated recycled materials and passive solar heating and her innovative financing model, together
A. The paired dashes correctly set off "which incorporated recycled materials and passive solar heating" as a parenthetical describing "the design." The comma after "model" separates the compound subject ("the design...and her innovative financing model") from the predicate ("together made the project viable"). The punctuation is correct. C erroneously expands the dashed section to include "her innovative financing model" — but that's part of the main sentence subject, not the parenthetical.

Summary card
Keep this
Dashes — Everything you need
Single dash
Dramatic pause before an elaboration, explanation, or revelation at the end of a sentence. More emphatic than a colon.
Paired dashes
Frame a parenthetical insertion mid-sentence. Must match — dash opens, dash closes. Never dash + comma or comma + dash.
The pairing rule
If you see an opening dash, the closing punctuation MUST also be a dash. Mixed pairs (dash + comma) are always wrong on the SAT.
Test it
Remove the dashed phrase. Does the sentence still make complete sense? If yes, the dashes correctly frame a parenthetical.
