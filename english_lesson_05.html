Agree & Extend
These questions give you two short passages on the same topic. The relationship here: the authors broadly agree, or one author builds further in the direction the other pointed.
Part of ~3–4 cross-text questionsTarget: 90 secPre-step: write a sentence for each passage first
🤝
What is Agreement / Extension?
Step 1
Two relationship types covered here

Agreement: Both authors make the same or very similar claim — though they may use different evidence, examples, or framing. Neither contradicts the other. Both reach compatible conclusions.

Extension: Passage 2 takes Passage 1's claim further in the same direction. Passage 1 says "X." Passage 2 says "X — and furthermore, Y." The second author doesn't challenge; they deepen or broaden.

These are the "positive relationship" types. The questions that test them typically ask: "Which claim would Author 2 most likely agree with?" or "How does Passage 2 relate to Passage 1?" or "Both authors would agree that..."

Agreement vs Extension — the distinction

Agreement: P1 says "Exercise improves mental health." P2 says "Physical activity is beneficial for psychological wellbeing." Same claim, different phrasing, possibly different evidence. Neither adds scope.

Extension: P1 says "Exercise improves mental health." P2 says "Exercise improves mental health — and specifically, aerobic exercise produces neurochemical changes that reduce anxiety at the cellular level." P2 accepts P1's claim and drills deeper into the mechanism.

The key test: does P2 say something P1 doesn't? If yes and it's compatible → extension. If no new scope → agreement.

⭐
The strategy — two sentences before the question
Step 2
1
Read Passage 1. Write one sentence: "Author 1 argues that ___." Be specific — what is the actual claim?
2
Read Passage 2. Write one sentence: "Author 2 argues that ___." Same specificity.
3
Compare your two sentences. Do they point in the same direction? Where does P2 go further or deeper? That overlap and extension is what the correct answer will describe.
4
For "both agree" questions: the correct answer must be a claim that BOTH sentences support. It will usually be more general than either author's specific claim. Eliminate anything one passage contradicts.
5
For "what would Author 2 say about Author 1's claim" questions: use only what Passage 2 actually says. Never infer beyond the text.
⚠️
The traps
Step 3
Trap 1
Too strong a claim
Both authors might support "X is beneficial." Wrong answer: "Both would agree that X is the most important factor in Y." Neither passage says "most important." Avoid superlatives in the correct answer.
Trap 2
Only one passage supports it
For "both agree" questions, the correct answer must be supported by BOTH passages. A choice accurate for P1 but not mentioned or contradicted by P2 is wrong.
Trap 3
Inferring beyond the text
What would Author 2 "likely" think? Only what Passage 2 actually says. Don't project views the author might plausibly hold but didn't state.
Trap 4
Confusing extension with contradiction
P2 adds new information that P1 didn't include. A distractor might say P2 "challenges" P1 — but adding scope is not challenging. Extension is always compatible with the original claim.

Worked examples
Step 4
Example 1 — AgreementMedium
Passage 1: Sleep deprivation has well-documented effects on cognitive performance. Studies show that individuals sleeping fewer than six hours per night perform significantly worse on tasks requiring sustained attention, working memory, and executive function than those meeting recommended sleep guidelines. The impairment is comparable to that produced by moderate alcohol intoxication.

Passage 2: The economic costs of insufficient sleep are substantial. Lost productivity, increased error rates, and higher rates of workplace accidents in sleep-deprived populations cost the US economy an estimated $411 billion per year, according to a RAND Corporation analysis. Addressing sleep deficits, researchers suggest, should be treated as a public health priority rather than a personal matter.
💭 Two-sentence summary
P1: Sleep deprivation significantly impairs cognitive performance.
P2: Sleep deprivation has major economic costs and should be treated as a public health issue.
Overlap: both say sleep deprivation is a serious problem with significant consequences.
Both passages support which of the following conclusions?
  • A. Sleep deprivation is primarily a problem for people in demanding professional roles
  • B. Insufficient sleep has significant negative consequences that extend beyond the individual
  • C. Governments should mandate minimum sleep hours for workers
  • D. Alcohol and sleep deprivation have identical effects on the brain
Answer: B. P1 shows cognitive impairment (consequences beyond the individual sleeper). P2 shows economic/societal costs. Both support: sleep deprivation's consequences extend beyond the individual. A (professional roles) — neither passage specifies this. C (government mandate) — only P2 calls for public health action, and doesn't specify mandates. D (identical effects) — P1 says "comparable to," not "identical."
Example 2 — ExtensionMedium
Passage 1: Urban tree canopy coverage has been shown to reduce ambient temperatures in city neighbourhoods, with studies finding that areas with higher tree density are consistently cooler during heat events than comparable areas without vegetation.

Passage 2: The benefits of urban trees extend well beyond temperature reduction. Research has documented improvements in air quality, stormwater management, and residents' mental health in high-canopy neighbourhoods. Notably, the mental health benefits — including reduced rates of anxiety and depression — appear to accrue even among residents who do not consciously engage with the green spaces, suggesting that passive exposure to vegetation affects wellbeing.
💭 Two-sentence summary
P1: Urban trees reduce temperatures.
P2: Urban trees have many other benefits beyond temperature reduction (air quality, stormwater, mental health).
Relationship: P2 extends P1 — accepts the temperature finding and adds more scope.
How does Passage 2 relate to the argument in Passage 1?
  • A. It contradicts Passage 1 by showing that temperature reduction is not the primary benefit of urban trees
  • B. It extends Passage 1 by demonstrating that the benefits of urban trees are broader than temperature reduction alone
  • C. It qualifies Passage 1 by noting that tree canopy is only effective in certain types of urban environments
  • D. It challenges the methodology used in Passage 1's temperature studies
Answer: B. P2 explicitly says "benefits extend well beyond temperature reduction" — it accepts P1's finding and adds to it. A (contradicts) — wrong direction. C (qualifies) — P2 doesn't limit the findings, it expands them. D (challenges methodology) — no such challenge is made.
Example 3 — What would Author 2 say?Hard
Passage 1: Economist Tyler Cowen argues that the internet has democratised access to cultural goods — music, art, literature — that were previously accessible only to those with the means and proximity to cultural institutions. For the first time in history, a teenager in rural Nebraska has access to the same catalogue of music as a resident of Manhattan.

Passage 2: While the digital distribution of cultural goods has expanded access in certain respects, it has also concentrated economic power in the hands of platform intermediaries. Streaming services, for instance, control which artists gain visibility through algorithmic promotion, and the payment structures they impose mean that even widely streamed musicians earn a fraction of what physical record sales would have generated. The gains in access have come at the cost of economic power for creators.
Author 2 would most likely respond to Cowen's argument in Passage 1 by saying that:
  • A. Cowen is wrong that digital distribution has expanded access to cultural goods
  • B. The economic model of streaming is superior to physical record sales for artists
  • C. While expanded access is a real benefit, it has been accompanied by a loss of economic power for the creators whose work is being accessed
  • D. The democratisation of cultural access is not genuinely beneficial because rural audiences cannot appreciate art the same way urban audiences do
Answer: C. P2 starts with "while... has expanded access in certain respects" — it accepts Cowen's point. Then it adds: economic power went to platforms at the expense of creators. C captures both the concession (access is real) and the critique (economic cost). A says access wasn't expanded — P2 accepts that it was. B praises streaming — P2 criticises its payment model. D invents a claim about rural audiences that P2 never makes.

Practice — 5 questions
Step 5
Practice 1
Passage 1: Bilingual children consistently outperform monolingual peers on tasks measuring executive function — specifically, the ability to switch between tasks and to inhibit irrelevant information. Managing two languages appears to exercise cognitive control systems in ways that produce measurable benefits.

Passage 2: The cognitive advantages of bilingualism may extend into old age. Longitudinal studies suggest that bilingual adults develop symptoms of Alzheimer's disease an average of 4–5 years later than monolingual adults with equivalent levels of education and socioeconomic status, leading researchers to hypothesise that managing two languages builds cognitive reserve that delays dementia onset.
Both passages support which of the following conclusions?
  • A. Bilingualism is primarily beneficial during childhood
  • B. All bilingual individuals will avoid developing Alzheimer's disease
  • C. Managing two languages produces cognitive benefits that extend across the lifespan
  • D. Monolingual individuals have inferior intelligence compared to bilingual individuals
C. P1: benefits in childhood (executive function). P2: benefits in old age (delayed dementia). Together: benefits span the lifespan. A (primarily childhood) — P2 explicitly extends to old age. B (all will avoid Alzheimer's) — too strong; P2 says delayed onset, not prevention. D (inferior intelligence) — neither passage makes this claim.
Practice 2
Passage 1: Community gardens in urban areas provide residents with access to fresh produce that is often unavailable or unaffordable in food-insecure neighbourhoods. Several cities have documented significant reductions in food insecurity among households that participate in community gardening programmes.

Passage 2: Beyond nutritional benefits, community gardens function as social infrastructure. Longitudinal studies of urban garden projects have found that participants report stronger feelings of community belonging, higher levels of social trust, and lower rates of social isolation compared to non-participants — effects that persist even when controlling for self-selection.
How does Passage 2 relate to the argument in Passage 1?
  • A. It contradicts Passage 1 by showing that the nutritional benefits of community gardens are overstated
  • B. It extends Passage 1 by identifying social benefits of community gardens beyond the nutritional ones Passage 1 describes
  • C. It qualifies Passage 1 by noting that community gardens only benefit participants, not the broader neighbourhood
  • D. It challenges the methodology of the studies cited in Passage 1
B. P2 begins "beyond nutritional benefits" — explicitly accepting P1's nutritional finding and extending to social benefits. A (contradicts) — P2 doesn't challenge the nutritional claim. C (only participants) — P2 says benefits are for participants, which doesn't limit P1's claims. D (challenges methodology) — no such challenge.
Practice 3 — Hard
Passage 1: Philosopher Martha Nussbaum argues that the study of literature is essential to democratic citizenship because fiction uniquely cultivates the imaginative capacity to understand lives different from one's own — what she calls "narrative imagination." Citizens who cannot imagine the perspective of others, she argues, are poorly equipped to make just decisions about people whose circumstances they have never experienced.

Passage 2: Empirical research has provided some support for Nussbaum's theoretical claims. Studies using randomised designs have found that reading literary fiction — as distinct from popular genre fiction or nonfiction — produces measurable short-term improvements in theory of mind: the ability to attribute mental states to others and to understand that others' beliefs and desires differ from one's own.
Author 2 would most likely characterise the relationship between the passages as:
  • A. Passage 2 refutes Nussbaum's claim by showing that genre fiction is equally effective
  • B. Passage 2 proves definitively that reading fiction makes people better democratic citizens
  • C. Passage 2 provides empirical evidence that partially supports Nussbaum's theoretical argument about fiction and perspective-taking
  • D. Passage 2 contradicts Nussbaum by showing that nonfiction is more effective than literary fiction
C. P2 says research provides "some support" for Nussbaum's "theoretical claims" — partial, empirical support. C matches exactly. A (genre fiction equally effective) — P2 says literary fiction works BETTER than genre. B (proves definitively) — too strong; P2 shows "short-term improvements" in theory of mind, not full democratic citizenship. D (nonfiction better) — the opposite of what P2 says.
Practice 4
Passage 1: Access to green space in cities has been associated with lower rates of depression and anxiety among urban residents. Studies comparing neighbourhoods with high and low park availability have consistently found better mental health outcomes in greener areas, even after controlling for income and other demographic variables.

Passage 2: The mental health benefits of urban green space appear to vary by the type of nature encountered. Research suggests that "blue spaces" — areas featuring water, such as rivers, lakes, and coastlines — produce stronger and faster mental health improvements than equivalent green spaces without water features. The sound and visual movement of water may engage stress-reduction pathways more effectively than static vegetation.
Both passages support which of the following?
  • A. All urban residents benefit equally from access to green space
  • B. Blue spaces are more effective than green spaces for mental health
  • C. Contact with natural environments in urban areas has mental health benefits
  • D. City planners should prioritise water features over parks in urban design
C. Both passages support that nature contact helps mental health — P1 via green space, P2 via blue spaces. C is the safe overlap. A (benefit equally) — neither passage says this. B (blue better than green) — this is P2's finding only, not supported by P1. D (policy recommendation) — neither passage explicitly recommends this policy.
Practice 5 — Hard
Passage 1: Early childhood interventions — programmes that provide nutritional support, cognitive stimulation, and parental education to disadvantaged children under five — have shown significant long-term returns. Studies tracking participants into adulthood have found higher rates of graduation, employment, and health, and lower rates of incarceration, compared to control groups. The economist James Heckman estimates the return on such investments at $7–12 for every dollar spent.

Passage 2: The evidence for early childhood investment is compelling, but the distribution of those investments matters enormously. Programmes targeted narrowly at the highest-risk families produce larger per-child returns than universal programmes, because the children who benefit most are those who would otherwise have the worst outcomes. A dollar spent on the most disadvantaged child has a greater effect on lifetime outcomes than the same dollar spent on a moderately disadvantaged one.
Author 2 would most likely respond to the evidence in Passage 1 by:
  • A. Arguing that Heckman's return estimates are exaggerated
  • B. Questioning whether early childhood programmes actually improve adult outcomes
  • C. Accepting the case for early childhood investment while adding that targeting the highest-risk children maximises the return on those investments
  • D. Arguing that universal programmes are superior to targeted ones because they reach more children
C. P2 opens "the evidence for early childhood investment is compelling" — accepts P1. Then adds: targeting the most disadvantaged maximises returns. C is the extension: agree with P1, then add targeting detail. A (exaggerated estimates) — P2 calls the evidence "compelling," not exaggerated. B (questions outcomes) — P2 accepts P1's conclusion. D (universal superior) — P2 argues the opposite: targeted beats universal per dollar.

Summary card
Keep this
Cross-Text: Agree & Extend — Everything you need
Non-negotiable pre-step
Write one sentence for each passage before reading any question. "Author 1 argues ___. Author 2 argues ___." Without this, choices all seem plausible.
Agreement
Both authors reach compatible conclusions, possibly with different evidence or framing. Neither adds scope or challenges the other.
Extension
P2 accepts P1's claim AND adds new scope, depth, or application. "X — and furthermore, Y." Signal word often: "beyond," "moreover," "additionally."
Trap 1
Too strong — "proves definitively," "most important," "all." Neither passage usually makes such absolute claims.
Trap 2
"Both agree" = must be supported by BOTH passages. Check each option against each passage individually.
Trap 3
Never infer beyond the text. "Author 2 would likely think..." — use only what P2 explicitly says. What the author might plausibly believe is irrelevant.
