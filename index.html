Part 1: Craft & Structure
28% of RW · ~13–15 questions
What this domain tests

Craft & Structure questions ask you to analyze how a text works — not just what it says. The SAT wants to know if you understand an author's choices: why they used a specific word, how they organized their argument, what their purpose is, and how two texts relate to each other.

The key mindset shift: you're reading like a critic, not a student taking notes.

Words in Context
Asks what a word or phrase most nearly means as used in the passage. The word is usually common but used in an unusual way.
~4–5 questions
Text Structure & Purpose
Asks why the author wrote the passage, or why a specific paragraph/sentence is included.
~3–4 questions
Cross-Text Connections
Two short passages. Asks how they relate — agree, disagree, support, complicate each other.
~3–4 questions
Author's Craft
Asks about tone, point of view, rhetorical choices — why the author used a technique.
~2–3 questions
Type 1 Words in Context

The trap: Students pick the most common definition of the word. The SAT always chooses a word that has an unusual meaning in context. Substitution is your only reliable tool.

The substitution strategy (always use this)

1. Cover the answer choices. Read the sentence. In your own words, what should go in that blank?
2. Now look at the choices. Pick the one closest to YOUR word.
3. Sub it back in. Does the sentence still make sense? Does it match the author's tone?

Example passage
Neuroscientist Lisa Feldman Barrett's theory of constructed emotion challenges the classical view that emotions are hardwired biological responses. Barrett argues that the brain does not passively receive emotional signals; rather, it actively constructs emotional experiences based on past experiences and current context. Her work suggests that emotions are not universal, fixed categories but flexible predictions the brain makes about what is happening in the body.
As used in the passage, "constructs" most nearly means —
  • A. builds physical structures in the environment
  • B. misinterprets incoming sensory data
  • C. actively creates or generates based on prior knowledge
  • D. records observations in an objective manner
Answer: C. "Constructs" here doesn't mean building something physical (A). The passage says the brain is active, not passive — it uses past experiences to generate emotional experiences. "Creates based on prior knowledge" captures this perfectly. The passage explicitly rules out D ("not passively receive").
Type 2 Text Structure & Purpose

The trap: Students summarize what the paragraph says instead of explaining why it's there. The question asks for the author's reason for including something, not a summary of its content.

The "why is this here?" strategy

Ask: What would the passage lose if this sentence/paragraph were deleted? That function is the answer. Common functions: introduce a counterargument, provide evidence, qualify a claim, establish context, illustrate with an example, transition between ideas.

Example passage
Scientists have long debated whether Neanderthals were capable of symbolic thought. Early analyses of their tools suggested only functional, utilitarian use. However, recent discoveries of Neanderthal cave art in Spain, dated to over 65,000 years ago — predating the arrival of modern humans in Europe — have shifted the conversation significantly. These findings imply that symbolic behavior may have evolved independently in Neanderthals, not as a trait borrowed from Homo sapiens.
The underlined sentence primarily serves to —
  • A. summarize the findings of archaeologists working in Spain
  • B. introduce evidence that challenges an earlier assumption
  • C. explain why scientists originally doubted Neanderthal intelligence
  • D. describe the artistic techniques used by Neanderthals
Answer: B. The previous sentence established the "early" view (only utilitarian tools). The underlined sentence begins with "However" — a signal word meaning contrast — and introduces cave art evidence that directly challenges that earlier view. A is too narrow (just summarizing), C is backward (it's challenging doubt, not explaining it), D is off-topic.
Type 3 Cross-Text Connections

The trap: Students try to synthesize two complex passages without a clear framework. You must identify each author's main claim first, then figure out the relationship between those claims.

The two-sentence summary strategy

Before looking at the question, write (or mentally state): "Author 1 argues that ___. Author 2 argues that ___." Then the relationship becomes obvious: do they agree? Partly agree? Directly contradict? Does one provide evidence for the other?

Example — two passages
Passage 1: Urban green spaces — parks, community gardens, tree-lined streets — have measurable benefits for residents' mental health. Studies show that even brief exposure to natural environments reduces cortisol levels and self-reported stress. Cities that invest in green infrastructure therefore improve public health outcomes alongside aesthetic ones.

Passage 2: The mental health benefits attributed to urban green spaces may be overstated. Many studies in this area rely on self-reported wellbeing data, which is notoriously inconsistent. Furthermore, access to green space is unequally distributed — wealthier neighborhoods typically have more parkland, meaning studies may actually be measuring the effects of socioeconomic advantage rather than nature exposure itself.
How does Passage 2 relate to the argument made in Passage 1?
  • A. It provides additional evidence that supports Passage 1's central claim
  • B. It argues that green spaces have no measurable effect on public health
  • C. It raises methodological and equity concerns that complicate Passage 1's conclusions
  • D. It shifts focus from mental health to economic factors entirely
Answer: C. Passage 2 doesn't say green spaces have no effect (B is too strong), and it doesn't provide support (A). It questions the methodology (self-reported data) and raises an equity concern (socioeconomic confounding) — both of which complicate, but don't fully refute, Passage 1's claims. D overstates the shift; economics is raised as a confound, not a new focus.

Part 2: Expression of Ideas
20% of RW · ~8–12 questions
What this domain tests

Expression of Ideas questions ask you to improve a piece of writing — not fix grammar (that's Standard English Conventions), but make it clearer, more logical, and better organized. You're acting as an editor.

The key mindset: you're asking "does this make the writing better?" not "is this grammatically correct?"

Transitions
Asks which transition word or phrase best connects two ideas. Tests whether you understand the logical relationship between sentences.
~4–5 questions
Rhetorical synthesis
Gives you notes or bullet points. Asks you to combine them into a sentence that accomplishes a specific purpose.
~3–4 questions
Add/delete/revise
Asks whether a sentence should be added, deleted, or changed — and why. Tests whether content is relevant, complete, and logical.
~2–3 questions
Type 1 Transitions

The trap: Students pick transitions that "sound right" without testing the logical relationship. "However," "therefore," "furthermore," and "for example" are all common — but only one fits any given relationship.

The relationship map (memorize these 5)

Contrast (opposing ideas): however, nevertheless, on the other hand, yet, despite this
Cause/Result (one leads to the other): therefore, thus, consequently, as a result
Addition (more of the same): furthermore, additionally, moreover, in addition
Example (illustrating a claim): for example, for instance, specifically, namely
Concession (admitting a point): although, while, even though, admittedly

Example
Early studies on multitasking suggested that humans could efficiently handle multiple cognitive tasks simultaneously. _________, subsequent research using neuroimaging revealed that the brain largely switches rapidly between tasks rather than processing them in parallel, with measurable costs to accuracy and reaction time.
Which choice best completes the text?
  • A. Furthermore
  • B. However
  • C. For example
  • D. Consequently
Answer: B. The first sentence says multitasking is efficient. The second says it actually has costs. These ideas contrast — so we need a contrast transition. "However" is the clearest contrast signal. "Furthermore" (A) adds more of the same idea — wrong. "For example" (C) would mean the second sentence illustrates the first — wrong, it contradicts it. "Consequently" (D) implies cause-and-effect — wrong.
Type 2 Rhetorical Synthesis

The trap: Students find an answer that uses all the facts from the notes, rather than one that specifically accomplishes the stated goal. Read the goal first — it's your filter.

Goal-first strategy

1. Read the goal sentence carefully. Underline the key verb: "emphasize the contrast," "illustrate the benefit," "explain the cause."
2. Eliminate any answer that doesn't do that specific thing — even if it's accurate.
3. Among remaining choices, pick the one most directly and efficiently accomplishing the goal.

Example — student notes
A student is writing a report on the decline of monarch butterflies and has taken the following notes:

• Monarch butterfly populations have declined by approximately 80% over the past two decades
• Loss of milkweed, the only plant monarchs lay eggs on, is a primary driver of decline
• Milkweed has been eliminated by herbicide use in agricultural areas
• Climate change is shifting the timing of monarch migration
• Conservation efforts include creating milkweed corridors along highways
The student wants to emphasize a direct cause-and-effect relationship between agricultural practices and monarch decline. Which choice most effectively uses the notes to accomplish this goal?
  • A. Monarch butterfly populations have dropped by about 80% over two decades, and conservation groups are responding by planting milkweed along highway corridors.
  • B. Multiple factors, including climate change and habitat loss, have contributed to the significant decline of monarch butterfly populations in recent years.
  • C. The widespread use of herbicides in agricultural areas has eliminated milkweed — the only plant monarchs use for reproduction — directly contributing to an 80% population decline.
  • D. Monarchs are endangered by climate change, which disrupts their migration patterns and reduces their ability to find suitable habitat.
Answer: C. The goal is a "direct cause-and-effect between agricultural practices and decline." C explicitly chains: herbicides → milkweed loss → reproduction failure → population decline. A mentions conservation (not the goal). B is vague and lists multiple causes without establishing the specific chain. D focuses on climate change, not agricultural practices.
Type 3 Add / Delete / Revise

The trap: Students add sentences because they're interesting or true, not because they help the passage achieve its specific purpose. The test always gives you a stated reason to evaluate against.

The relevance test

For "should this be added?" — ask: does this sentence directly support the passage's main point? Does the passage flow naturally without it? Information that's true but off-topic should be deleted or not added.

For "should this be deleted?" — ask: does removing this sentence create a gap in logic? If the passage makes perfect sense without it, delete it.

Example
The deep ocean floor, once thought to be a biological desert, is now recognized as one of Earth's most diverse ecosystems. Organisms have adapted to survive extreme pressure, total darkness, and near-freezing temperatures. [The Pacific Ocean is the largest and deepest ocean on Earth, covering more area than all of the world's landmasses combined.] Scientists have discovered entirely new species in hydrothermal vent communities, where chemosynthetic bacteria form the base of the food web rather than photosynthetic plants.
The writer is considering whether to keep or delete the bracketed sentence. Should the sentence be kept or deleted?
  • A. Kept, because it provides relevant context about where deep-sea organisms live
  • B. Kept, because it supports the claim that the ocean contains diverse ecosystems
  • C. Deleted, because it introduces information about ocean size that is unrelated to the passage's focus on deep-sea biodiversity
  • D. Deleted, because it contradicts information presented earlier in the passage
Answer: C. The passage is about deep-sea biodiversity and adaptation. The bracketed sentence gives a geography fact (Pacific Ocean is the largest) — true but irrelevant to the topic. The passage flows perfectly without it. D is wrong because there's no contradiction.

Craft & Structure Words in Context — Q7 to Q12
Passage for Q7–Q8
Philosopher Hannah Arendt argued that the banality of evil lies not in monstrous intent but in thoughtlessness — the failure to think critically about one's actions and their consequences. Her observations, drawn from the trial of Nazi bureaucrat Adolf Eichmann, suggested that ordinary people can perpetuate atrocities simply by following orders without moral reflection.
Q7. As used in the passage, "banality" most nearly means —
  • A. extreme cruelty
  • B. ordinary, unremarkable quality
  • C. deliberate concealment
  • D. political ambition
Answer: B. Arendt's argument is that evil is not extraordinary or monstrous (A) but mundane — arising from thoughtlessness. "Banality" means commonplace ordinariness. The passage reinforces this: "ordinary people," "simply by following orders."
Q8. As used in the passage, "perpetuate" most nearly means —
  • A. prevent from occurring
  • B. formally authorize
  • C. cause to continue or carry out
  • D. publicly acknowledge
Answer: C. "Perpetuate" means to make something continue. Ordinary people "carry out" atrocities through thoughtless compliance. A is the opposite. B and D don't fit the meaning of doing or continuing an action.
Passage for Q9–Q10
The reintroduction of gray wolves to Yellowstone in 1995 had cascading effects ecologists describe as a "trophic cascade." By culling elk populations, wolves altered the animals' grazing behavior, which allowed riverbank vegetation to recover. This precipitated a chain of changes: stabilized riverbanks, reduced erosion, and the return of beaver colonies.
Q9. As used in the passage, "culling" most nearly means —
  • A. domesticating and training
  • B. monitoring closely over time
  • C. selectively reducing in number
  • D. driving to a new habitat
Answer: C. Wolves as predators reduce prey populations by selectively hunting. The result — altered grazing behavior and vegetation recovery — only makes sense if the elk population was reduced. None of the other options produce those ecological effects.
Q10. As used in the passage, "precipitated" most nearly means —
  • A. slowed or delayed
  • B. described in scientific literature
  • C. brought about or triggered
  • D. reversed or undone
Answer: C. "Precipitated" means caused or triggered — the vegetation recovery set off a chain of further changes. A and D are opposite in meaning. B misses the causal meaning entirely.
Passage for Q11–Q12
In the early twentieth century, jazz musicians developed an idiomatic vocabulary of improvisation largely transmitted orally, from mentor to apprentice. Critics dismissed this tradition as ephemeral — too tied to live performance to constitute serious art — but recordings eventually preserved and legitimized the form.
Q11. As used in the passage, "idiomatic" most nearly means —
  • A. difficult to translate or understand
  • B. characteristic of a particular style or tradition
  • C. formally codified in written notation
  • D. borrowed from European classical music
Answer: B. "Idiomatic" in music means characteristic of a particular genre — the unique language of jazz improvisation. C is contradicted by the passage (it was oral, not written). D contradicts the framing of jazz as a distinct tradition.
Q12. As used in the passage, "ephemeral" most nearly means —
  • A. technically complex
  • B. commercially unsuccessful
  • C. morally questionable
  • D. fleeting, existing only briefly
Answer: D. Critics argued jazz existed only in live performance — once the moment passed, it was gone. "Ephemeral" means short-lived or fleeting. The rest confirms this: recordings were needed to "preserve" it, implying it would otherwise disappear.
Craft & Structure Text Structure & Purpose — Q13 to Q16
Passage for Q13–Q14
Plastic pollution in marine environments has reached alarming levels. A 2022 study found that microplastics — particles smaller than 5 millimeters — are now present in the deepest trenches of the Pacific Ocean, thousands of meters below the surface. Researchers are particularly concerned about filter feeders such as whales and manta rays, which ingest large volumes of seawater daily and cannot easily expel plastic particles.
Q13. The underlined sentence primarily functions to —
  • A. introduce a counterargument to the opening claim
  • B. provide specific evidence that supports the opening claim
  • C. shift focus from ocean depth to marine animals
  • D. explain how microplastics are formed
Answer: B. The opening sentence claims plastic pollution is at "alarming levels." The underlined sentence provides a concrete data point (2022 study, deepest trenches) that supports and illustrates that alarm. It doesn't contradict, shift focus, or explain formation.
Q14. The main purpose of the final sentence (about filter feeders) is to —
  • A. summarize all the ways plastic harms the ocean
  • B. provide a definition of filter feeding
  • C. illustrate a specific biological consequence of the pollution described
  • D. argue that filter feeders are more important than other marine animals
Answer: C. The sentence narrows from the general problem (microplastics everywhere) to a specific harm on a species. It illustrates one consequence — not summarizes all of them (A), not defines (B), not ranks animals (D).
Passage for Q15–Q16
Traditional economic models assume that consumers make rational decisions based on complete information. Yet studies in behavioral economics consistently show that people make systematic, predictable errors — buying more when items are labeled "sale" regardless of actual savings, or avoiding losses far more strongly than they seek equivalent gains. These findings have reshaped how policymakers design retirement savings plans and organ donation programs.
Q15. The underlined sentence serves primarily to —
  • A. define the key terms used in behavioral economics
  • B. challenge the assumption in the opening sentence with empirical evidence
  • C. explain why traditional economic models were created
  • D. argue that consumers are incapable of rational thought
Answer: B. The opening states the traditional assumption (rational consumers). The underlined sentence begins with "Yet" — a contrast signal — and provides study evidence of irrational behavior, directly challenging that assumption. D is too extreme; the passage says "systematic errors," not total irrationality.
Q16. The overall structure of this passage is best described as —
  • A. a problem followed by multiple failed solutions
  • B. a historical narrative tracing the development of economics
  • C. a traditional claim, evidence against it, and a real-world implication
  • D. two competing theories with no resolution
Answer: C. Sentence 1 = traditional claim. Sentence 2 = evidence against it. Sentence 3 = real-world implication (policy redesign). This classic three-part structure is very common in SAT passages. The passage does resolve toward behavioral economics, so D is wrong.
Craft & Structure Cross-Text Connections — Q17 to Q19
Passages for Q17–Q19
Passage 1: Remote work has proven broadly beneficial for knowledge workers. Studies show that employees working from home report higher job satisfaction, fewer interruptions, and greater autonomy. Productivity metrics in many sectors have either held steady or improved since the widespread adoption of remote work after 2020.

Passage 2: The productivity gains attributed to remote work may obscure important costs. Junior employees and new hires lose access to informal mentorship and spontaneous collaboration that occur naturally in offices. Over time, these deficits may erode institutional knowledge and impede career development for workers who lack established professional networks.
Q17. Which best describes the relationship between the two passages?
  • A. Passage 2 directly contradicts the productivity data cited in Passage 1
  • B. Passage 2 argues that remote work should be eliminated entirely
  • C. Passage 2 acknowledges the benefits in Passage 1 but highlights costs not captured by productivity metrics
  • D. Passage 2 focuses on a completely different topic than Passage 1
Answer: C. Passage 2 doesn't dispute the productivity data (A) — it says those gains "may obscure" other costs. It doesn't call for elimination (B). It adds dimensions (mentorship, career development) that productivity numbers don't capture — a "yes, but also" relationship.
Q18. The author of Passage 1 would most likely respond to Passage 2's concern about junior employees by —
  • A. agreeing that remote work is harmful for all workers
  • B. noting that Passage 2's concern involves factors outside the productivity and satisfaction metrics Passage 1 cites
  • C. arguing that junior employees prefer remote work more than senior employees
  • D. claiming that informal mentorship is overrated as a career development tool
Answer: B. Passage 1's evidence covers job satisfaction and productivity. Passage 2's concern is mentorship and career development — different metrics. The most defensible response from Passage 1's author is that their data doesn't address those dimensions. A, C, and D introduce claims not found in Passage 1.
Q19. Both passages would most likely agree that —
  • A. remote work should be made mandatory across all industries
  • B. remote work became widespread following 2020
  • C. productivity is the only meaningful measure of workplace success
  • D. offices are superior environments for all workers
Answer: B. Passage 1 explicitly mentions "widespread adoption after 2020," and Passage 2 discusses the current state of remote work on the same implied timeline. Neither argues for mandating remote work (A), Passage 2 explicitly disputes productivity as the only metric (C), and D is the position of neither author.
Expression of Ideas Transitions — Q20 to Q22
Q20. Which transition best completes the text?

Sleep deprivation impairs cognitive function, reaction time, and emotional regulation. _______, chronic sleep loss has been linked to long-term risks including cardiovascular disease and metabolic disorders.
  • A. However
  • B. For example
  • C. Furthermore
  • D. Therefore
Answer: C. The first sentence lists short-term harms. The second adds long-term harms — more of the same category. This is an addition relationship. "Furthermore" adds information. "However" is wrong (no contrast). "Therefore" is wrong (no cause-effect between the two sentences).
Q21. Which transition best completes the text?

Early conservationists believed the best way to protect nature was to preserve it entirely from human interference. _______, contemporary conservation biology increasingly recognizes that some human management — controlled burns, invasive species removal — is necessary to maintain healthy ecosystems.
  • A. Similarly
  • B. As a result
  • C. In contrast
  • D. In other words
Answer: C. First sentence: no human interference. Second sentence: human management is necessary. Opposing views = contrast. "Similarly" (A) implies agreement. "As a result" (B) would mean the new view was caused by the old — not stated. "In other words" (D) would mean the second rephrases the first — it doesn't.
Q22. Which transition best completes the text?

Researchers had long assumed the Amazon rainforest was a pristine, unmanaged wilderness. _______, archaeological evidence of ancient settlements, agricultural terracing, and earthworks suggests that indigenous peoples significantly shaped the landscape for thousands of years.
  • A. Consequently
  • B. Additionally
  • C. Nevertheless
  • D. Specifically
Answer: C. The first sentence states a prior assumption (pristine). The second presents contradicting evidence (human shaping). "Nevertheless" signals that the evidence stands against the assumption. "Consequently" (A) = cause-effect (wrong). "Additionally" (B) = adds more of the same (wrong, it opposes). "Specifically" (D) = narrows the first claim (wrong).
Expression of Ideas Rhetorical Synthesis — Q23 to Q25
Notes for Q23–Q24
A student researching the history of the internet has gathered these notes:

• ARPANET, the precursor to the internet, was developed in 1969 by the U.S. Department of Defense
• Initial purpose: allow military computers to communicate even if part of the network was destroyed
• By the 1980s, universities used it to share academic research
• Tim Berners-Lee invented the World Wide Web in 1989, enabling non-technical users to navigate the internet
• Commercial internet service providers began offering public access in the early 1990s
Q23. The student wants to emphasize how the internet's original purpose differed from its eventual public use. Which choice best accomplishes this goal?
  • A. Tim Berners-Lee's invention of the World Wide Web in 1989 made the internet accessible to ordinary users for the first time.
  • B. The internet has its origins in ARPANET, developed in 1969 and later expanded to universities in the 1980s.
  • C. Though originally designed by the U.S. Department of Defense for military resilience, the internet evolved into a public communications network accessible to ordinary users by the 1990s.
  • D. ARPANET was created so that military computers could continue to communicate in the event of a network disruption.
Answer: C. The goal is to show the contrast between original purpose (military resilience) and eventual public use. C sets up that contrast explicitly with "though originally... evolved into." A only covers one end. B summarizes expansion without contrasting purposes. D only covers the military origin — half the story.
Q24. The student wants to present the development of the internet as a sequence of expanding access. Which choice best accomplishes this goal?
  • A. The internet's history is complicated by military origins that many users today are unaware of.
  • B. Access to the internet expanded from military networks in 1969, to universities in the 1980s, and finally to the general public in the early 1990s.
  • C. Tim Berners-Lee's World Wide Web was the most important development in internet history because it made navigation simple for non-technical users.
  • D. ARPANET and the modern internet serve very different purposes despite sharing the same underlying technology.
Answer: B. The goal is "expanding access as a sequence." B maps exactly onto that: military (1969) → universities (1980s) → public (1990s). A introduces a tone not in the notes. C focuses on one event and makes a judgment not in the notes. D is a contrast statement, not a sequence.
Notes for Q25
A student writing about color perception has gathered these notes:

• Human eyes contain two photoreceptor types: rods and cones
• Rods detect light and dark; cones detect color
• Three types of cones, each sensitive to different wavelengths: red, green, and blue
• Color blindness occurs when one or more cone types are absent or dysfunctional
• Approximately 8% of men and 0.5% of women have some form of color blindness
Q25. The student wants to explain color blindness by connecting it to the mechanism of normal color perception. Which choice best accomplishes this goal?
  • A. Color blindness is more common in men than in women, affecting approximately 8% of the male population.
  • B. Human eyes contain rods and cones, and the three types of cones are each sensitive to red, green, or blue wavelengths.
  • C. Color perception is a complex process involving multiple types of photoreceptor cells working together in the human eye.
  • D. Because normal color vision depends on three cone types sensitive to red, green, and blue wavelengths, a deficiency or absence of any one type results in the condition known as color blindness.
Answer: D. The goal is to explain color blindness by connecting it to normal perception. D chains: three cone types → normal vision → deficiency in one → color blindness. A gives only prevalence statistics. B explains normal vision but doesn't mention color blindness. C is too vague and doesn't mention color blindness.

The two-sentence summary framework
Do this before every cross-text question
Why most students get these wrong

Students read both passages carefully, get overwhelmed by detail, and then try to evaluate four answer choices without a clear lens. The fix is mechanical: before reading the question, write or mentally state exactly two sentences:

The non-negotiable pre-step (30 seconds)

1. Read Passage 1. In one sentence: "Author 1 argues/claims/suggests that ___."
2. Read Passage 2. In one sentence: "Author 2 argues/claims/suggests that ___."
3. Now ask: what is the relationship between those two sentences?

Only after this do you look at the question and the answer choices. This single habit eliminates most cross-text errors.

The relationship between your two summary sentences is almost always the answer to the question. The answer choices are usually just four different labels for that relationship — three of which are wrong.

The five relationship types
Memorize all five
Type 1 — Direct agreement

Both authors make the same core claim, possibly using different evidence or framing. The SAT will disguise this by having the passages use different vocabulary or focus on different examples — but the underlying conclusion is the same.

Signal words to look for

Both passages: "supports," "corroborates," "confirms," "reaches the same conclusion," "similarly argues," "both suggest." Watch for answer choices that say one passage "undermines" or "contradicts" — those are wrong for this type.

Example
Passage 1: Marine biologist Dr. Sylvia Earle argues that ocean acidification poses the most immediate threat to coral reef ecosystems. As CO₂ dissolves into seawater, carbonate ions become less available — the exact building blocks corals need to construct their calcium carbonate skeletons.

Passage 2: A 2023 study in Nature Climate Change found that reefs exposed to higher acidity showed skeletal growth rates 40% below normal. The researchers concluded that without significant reductions in atmospheric CO₂, reef calcification will become physiologically impossible within decades.
How does Passage 2 relate to Passage 1?
  • A. It contradicts Earle's claim by showing that reefs can adapt to acidic conditions
  • B. It provides empirical evidence that supports Earle's argument about the threat of ocean acidification
  • C. It shifts focus from acidification to CO₂ emissions as the primary cause of reef decline
  • D. It qualifies Earle's claim by suggesting the threat is less severe than she implies
Answer: B. Summary 1: "Acidification threatens reefs by depleting carbonate ions." Summary 2: "Acidification reduces skeletal growth by 40%." Both say acidification harms reefs — direct agreement. Passage 2 provides quantitative evidence (40%, "physiologically impossible") that supports and strengthens Passage 1's claim. C is wrong — CO₂ is mentioned as the cause of acidification in both, not a different threat.
Type 2 — Direct disagreement / contradiction

The two authors reach opposite conclusions about the same topic. One says X is true; the other says X is false (or that not-X is true). This is the most obvious relationship type — but students still get it wrong by choosing answer choices that are too strong (e.g., "completely refutes") when the disagreement is partial.

Calibrate the strength of the disagreement

SAT answers often include one "correct relationship, wrong intensity" trap. If Passage 2 says "evidence is mixed," that doesn't mean it "completely disproves" Passage 1. Eliminate choices that overstate or understate the conflict.

Example
Passage 1: Psychologist Carol Dweck's research on "growth mindset" — the belief that intelligence can be developed through effort — has shown consistent improvements in student achievement across diverse school populations. Her intervention studies demonstrate that teaching students about neuroplasticity motivates persistence and raises grades.

Passage 2: A 2019 meta-analysis examining over 400 growth mindset studies found that the effect sizes were far smaller than originally reported, with many studies showing negligible impacts on academic outcomes. The researchers suggested that publication bias and methodological weaknesses had inflated the original findings.
The relationship between Passage 1 and Passage 2 is best described as:
  • A. Passage 2 confirms the findings described in Passage 1 using a larger sample
  • B. Passage 2 extends Passage 1 by identifying the specific mechanisms behind growth mindset's success
  • C. Passage 2 challenges the claims in Passage 1 by questioning the reliability of the supporting evidence
  • D. Passage 2 completely invalidates Passage 1 by proving that growth mindset has no effect
Answer: C. Summary 1: "Growth mindset interventions reliably raise achievement." Summary 2: "Effect sizes are small; the evidence is methodologically weak." This is a challenge/contradiction — but note D is too strong. Passage 2 says effects are "negligible" and evidence is inflated, but does NOT say there is zero effect. C captures the right calibration: questioning reliability, not total invalidation.
Type 3 — Complication / qualification

Passage 2 doesn't directly disagree with Passage 1 — instead it introduces a nuance, an exception, or an additional factor that complicates the picture. This is the trickiest type because it's neither pure agreement nor pure disagreement. Passage 2 might say: "yes, but…" or "this is true, except when…" or "the claim holds, but only under certain conditions."

The "yes, but" test

If you can summarize the relationship as "Passage 2 says 'yes, Passage 1's claim is true, but it's more complicated than that'," you're looking at a complication. The SAT uses words like: "qualifies," "complicates," "nuances," "refines," "adds a caveat to," "raises questions about."

Example
Passage 1: Numerous studies confirm that exercise improves cognitive function in older adults. Regular aerobic activity increases hippocampal volume, enhances memory consolidation, and reduces the risk of dementia by up to 30%. Public health officials have championed exercise as a cost-effective cognitive intervention.

Passage 2: While the cognitive benefits of exercise are well-documented in the general population, recent research suggests these benefits may not apply equally across all groups. Individuals with certain genetic variants — particularly the APOE-ε4 allele, a known risk factor for Alzheimer's — showed minimal cognitive gains from aerobic training in a landmark 2022 clinical trial, raising questions about personalized approaches to dementia prevention.
How does Passage 2 relate to the argument in Passage 1?
  • A. It directly contradicts Passage 1 by showing that exercise does not improve cognition
  • B. It reinforces Passage 1 by providing additional evidence that exercise reduces dementia risk
  • C. It complicates Passage 1 by identifying a subgroup for whom the benefits may not apply
  • D. It shifts Passage 1's focus from exercise to genetic factors as the key to dementia prevention
Answer: C. Passage 2 opens with "while the cognitive benefits of exercise are well-documented" — it accepts Passage 1's general claim. But it introduces an exception: APOE-ε4 carriers don't benefit. This is a complication, not a contradiction (A would require Passage 2 to say exercise is universally ineffective) and not reinforcement (B). D overstates — genetics is raised as a moderating factor, not a replacement explanation.
Type 4 — Extension / elaboration

Passage 2 takes Passage 1's claim and adds new territory: a broader application, a deeper mechanism, a longer time horizon, or a new domain. Unlike complication, extension doesn't introduce doubt — it expands the scope of what Passage 1 said.

Extension vs. complication — the key distinction

Extension: Passage 2 adds MORE evidence or a WIDER scope in the same direction as Passage 1.
Complication: Passage 2 introduces a BUT, an EXCEPT, or a CAVEAT that limits Passage 1's scope.

Ask yourself: does Passage 2 make you MORE confident in Passage 1's claim (extension) or LESS confident (complication)?

Example
Passage 1: Historians have documented how the Black Death of 1347–1351 transformed European labor markets. With roughly one-third of the population dead, surviving workers could command higher wages, challenge feudal obligations, and in many cases abandon serfdom entirely.

Passage 2: The demographic disruption of the Black Death also reshaped European agriculture in the long term. As labor became scarce and expensive, landowners increasingly converted arable farmland to sheep pasture, which required fewer workers. This shift helped lay the groundwork for the English wool trade and, later, the early textile industry — connecting a fourteenth-century plague to the origins of industrial capitalism.
Passage 2 primarily serves to:
  • A. Contradict Passage 1's account of the Black Death's economic impact
  • B. Qualify Passage 1 by noting that labor market changes were less significant than believed
  • C. Extend Passage 1's argument by tracing additional long-term economic consequences of the same demographic disruption
  • D. Shift focus from labor markets to the cultural impact of the Black Death
Answer: C. Passage 1 establishes that population loss changed labor markets. Passage 2 takes the SAME starting point (labor scarcity from the plague) and follows it into new territory: agricultural conversion → wool trade → industrial capitalism. This adds scope and depth in the same direction — classic extension. No contradiction, no caveat, no cultural shift.
Type 5 — Methodological or evidential critique

Passage 2 doesn't dispute the conclusion of Passage 1 directly — instead, it questions how Passage 1 reached that conclusion. It might criticize the research methods, the sample size, the data source, or the logical reasoning. This is subtle: the two passages might actually agree on the conclusion but disagree on the evidence quality.

Watch for these critique patterns

• "The data relied upon by [Passage 1] is unreliable because…"
• "Correlation was mistaken for causation"
• "The sample was too small / not representative"
• "The study did not control for confounding variables"
• "Self-reported data is notoriously unreliable"

These critiques attack the evidence, not necessarily the conclusion. An answer choice that says Passage 2 "disproves" Passage 1 is usually too strong here.

Example
Passage 1: A decade-long study of 10,000 participants found that people who reported eating breakfast daily scored significantly higher on memory and attention tests than those who skipped it. The researchers concluded that breakfast consumption is causally linked to improved cognitive performance.

Passage 2: The claim that breakfast improves cognition rests on shaky empirical foundations. Most studies in this area — including large longitudinal ones — rely on self-reported dietary data, which participants complete inconsistently and inaccurately. More critically, these studies fail to adequately control for socioeconomic status: people who regularly eat breakfast also tend to have more stable lives, better sleep, and less chronic stress — all independent predictors of cognitive performance.
The author of Passage 2 would most likely critique Passage 1 on the grounds that:
  • A. The study's sample size was too small to draw meaningful conclusions
  • B. Breakfast has no effect on cognitive performance under any circumstances
  • C. The study's reliance on self-reported data and failure to control for confounding variables undermines its causal claims
  • D. The researchers focused on the wrong cognitive outcomes
Answer: C. Passage 2 raises two specific methodological problems: (1) self-reported data is unreliable, and (2) socioeconomic status is a confound that wasn't controlled for. These directly target Passage 1's causal claim. A is wrong — Passage 2 doesn't mention sample size (10,000 is actually large). B is too absolute — Passage 2 doesn't say breakfast never helps. D is not raised at all.

Three advanced question forms
Appear on every SAT
Form 1 — Point-of-view questions

"How would the author of Passage 1 respond to Passage 2?" or "What would Author 2 say about the claim in Passage 1?"

Strategy: answer from the text, not from logic

Only use what is actually stated or strongly implied in the passage. Do NOT infer what the author "might" think based on general knowledge. The correct answer is always directly supported by language in the passage. Eliminate any choice that introduces a claim not in the text.

Example — using the breakfast passages above
How would the author of Passage 1 most likely respond to Passage 2's critique about confounding variables?
  • A. By agreeing that the study should have included socioeconomic controls
  • B. By pointing out that the study was large (10,000 participants) and longitudinal, which helps account for variation across participants
  • C. By arguing that socioeconomic status is irrelevant to cognitive performance
  • D. By conceding that self-reported data makes causal conclusions impossible
Answer: B. Passage 1 would defend its methodology using the information it actually provides: a decade-long study of 10,000 participants. The author would argue that the scale and duration help account for variation — without necessarily conceding the confounding issue (A requires the author to agree with the critique, which Passage 1 doesn't do). C and D go beyond what Passage 1 states.
Form 2 — "Both agree / both would accept" questions

"Both authors would most likely agree that ___." These require you to find a claim supported by BOTH passages — usually something more general than either passage's main argument.

Strategy: find the overlap zone

The overlap is almost always: (1) a shared factual premise both passages mention, or (2) the question both passages are responding to. It is almost NEVER one author's conclusion — that would be one-sided. Eliminate any answer that one passage contradicts or doesn't address.

Example — using the growth mindset passages above
Both passages would most likely agree that:
  • A. Growth mindset interventions reliably improve student achievement
  • B. Growth mindset interventions have no meaningful effect on academic outcomes
  • C. Research on growth mindset has been conducted and published across diverse school populations
  • D. Publication bias has distorted most psychological research
Answer: C. A is only Passage 1's view — Passage 2 disputes it. B is only Passage 2's characterization — Passage 1 disputes it. D is only Passage 2's claim (not in Passage 1). C is neutral: Passage 1 describes research across "diverse school populations" and Passage 2 discusses "over 400 studies" — both acknowledge the research exists and is widely published. Safe overlap.
Form 3 — Hypothetical evidence questions

"If a researcher found that X, how would this affect the arguments in both passages?" These require you to reason about what new evidence would do to each author's position — strengthen, weaken, or leave unchanged.

Strategy: apply each piece of evidence to each author separately

Step 1: Would this evidence support or undermine Passage 1's claim?
Step 2: Would this evidence support or undermine Passage 2's claim?
Then find the answer choice that correctly describes BOTH effects simultaneously. Wrong answers usually get one right and one wrong.

Example — using the exercise/cognition passages above
Suppose researchers discover that among individuals without the APOE-ε4 allele, aerobic exercise reduces dementia risk by 45% — stronger than any previously documented intervention. How would this finding affect the arguments in both passages?
  • A. It would strengthen both passages equally
  • B. It would strengthen Passage 1's general case for exercise while also supporting Passage 2's point that benefits vary by genetic profile
  • C. It would weaken Passage 1 by showing that exercise only benefits a genetic subgroup
  • D. It would undermine Passage 2 by proving that exercise is universally effective
Answer: B. Apply separately: Passage 1 argues exercise improves cognition — a 45% reduction rate strengthens this. Passage 2 argues benefits vary by genetics — finding that NON-carriers benefit strongly (while carriers don't, per the original study) actually supports the personalized medicine angle. C is wrong because "only a subgroup" is too strong — Passage 1 never claimed universality. D is wrong because variation by genetics is consistent with, not contradicted by, non-carriers benefiting strongly.

Instructions

All 25 questions test cross-text connections. For each pair of passages, write your two-sentence summary before reading the question. Questions are grouped by relationship type so you can identify your weakest category.

Relationship type: Agreement — Q9 to Q13
Passages for Q9–Q11
Passage 1: Architect and urban theorist Jane Jacobs argued that neighborhoods thrive when they contain a mix of uses — housing, shops, workplaces, and public spaces — within walking distance of each other. This density and diversity of purpose creates what she called "eyes on the street": informal surveillance by residents that naturally deters crime and fosters community cohesion.

Passage 2: Contemporary urban data supports the idea that mixed-use neighborhoods are safer and more economically resilient than single-use zones. Studies of 40 American cities found that blocks with a higher ratio of commercial-to-residential properties had significantly lower property crime rates and higher rates of pedestrian activity — both indicators of the vibrant street life that characterizes healthy urban environments.
Q9. How does Passage 2 relate to the argument in Passage 1?
  • A. It challenges Jacobs' framework by showing that commercial density increases crime in some cities
  • B. It provides empirical evidence across multiple cities that supports Jacobs' theoretical claims about mixed-use design
  • C. It narrows Jacobs' argument by focusing only on property crime rather than community cohesion
  • D. It extends Jacobs' argument by exploring single-use zoning as an alternative
Answer: B. Passage 1: "Mixed-use neighborhoods are safer and more cohesive." Passage 2: "Data from 40 cities shows mixed-use areas have lower crime and more pedestrian activity." Direct agreement — Passage 2 provides the empirical support that validates Passage 1's theoretical argument. A contradicts what the passage says. C mischaracterizes (the study also covers pedestrian activity, which maps to "community cohesion"). D is wrong — single-use zoning is the contrast case, not an exploration.
Q10. Both authors would most likely agree that:
  • A. Single-use zoning is more economically efficient than mixed-use design
  • B. The presence of diverse activities in a neighborhood is associated with positive social outcomes
  • C. Informal surveillance is more effective than policing in reducing urban crime
  • D. Mixed-use design only works in large American cities
Answer: B. Passage 1: diverse uses → cohesion and safety. Passage 2: commercial-residential mix → lower crime and more foot traffic. Both affirm the connection between diversity of use and positive outcomes. A contradicts both passages. C is only from Passage 1 (and even then, it's about informal surveillance being A factor, not better than policing). D is too narrow — neither passage restricts the argument to large cities.
Q11. The author of Passage 1 would most likely view the study described in Passage 2 as:
  • A. A methodologically flawed study that misinterprets crime data
  • B. Evidence that contradicts her central argument about street life
  • C. Empirical confirmation of her theoretical framework for healthy neighborhood design
  • D. A study that is too narrowly focused on commercial properties to be useful
Answer: C. Jacobs' framework (Passage 1) predicts that mixed-use design produces safety and community life. The study (Passage 2) measures exactly those outcomes and finds them. From Jacobs' perspective, this is confirmation. There's no basis in either passage for A, B, or D.
Passages for Q12–Q13
Passage 1: Linguist George Lakoff argues that political framing shapes how citizens understand policy issues more powerfully than facts alone. When conservatives frame the estate tax as a "death tax," they activate emotional associations with injustice — paying taxes twice, once in life and again in death — that override statistical arguments about its narrow application to very wealthy estates.

Passage 2: Experimental research in political psychology confirms that the labels attached to policies influence public support independently of policy content. In one study, participants shown identical descriptions of a tax reduction supported it at 22% higher rates when it was labeled a "tax relief" measure compared to a "tax cut for the wealthy" — despite identical underlying policy details in both versions.
Q12. The relationship between Passage 1 and Passage 2 is best described as:
  • A. Passage 2 challenges Lakoff's claim by showing that voters respond to policy content, not framing
  • B. Passage 2 provides experimental evidence that corroborates Lakoff's theoretical argument about the power of political framing
  • C. Passage 2 extends Passage 1 by analyzing how framing affects media coverage rather than public opinion
  • D. Passage 2 qualifies Lakoff's argument by suggesting framing only works with tax-related policies
Answer: B. Passage 1: "Framing shapes understanding more powerfully than facts." Passage 2: "Identical policies get 22% different support based on label alone." Direct agreement — the experiment is exactly the kind of evidence that validates Lakoff's theoretical claim. A reverses the finding. C misidentifies what the study measures. D is too narrow — the study supports the general claim, not a tax-only limitation.
Q13. Which claim from Passage 1 is most directly supported by the study described in Passage 2?
  • A. Facts alone determine how citizens evaluate policies
  • B. The estate tax is narrowly applied to very wealthy estates
  • C. Political framing can override factual content in shaping public support
  • D. Emotional associations are more powerful than economic self-interest
Answer: C. The study in Passage 2 shows that identical policy content gets different support based on the label — the framing overrides the facts. This directly confirms Passage 1's claim that framing is "more powerful than facts alone." A is the opposite of what Passage 1 claims. B is a detail from Passage 1, not a claim the Passage 2 study addresses. D goes beyond what Passage 2 measures.
Relationship type: Contradiction / challenge — Q14 to Q18
Passages for Q14–Q16
Passage 1: Historian Jared Diamond's influential thesis in Guns, Germs, and Steel attributes the rise of European power to geographic and environmental advantages — access to domesticable animals, fertile land, and east-west continental axes that facilitated the spread of agriculture. In his framework, history's broad patterns are determined by ecology, not by the cultural or intellectual qualities of particular peoples.

Passage 2: Critics of environmental determinism argue that Diamond's framework oversimplifies the relationship between geography and power. Economic historian Deirdre McCloskey points out that identical geographic conditions produced vastly different outcomes across different centuries — the same fertile crescent that gave rise to early agriculture later became economically stagnant while Northwestern Europe surged ahead. Culture, institutions, and ideas, she argues, explain the divergences that geography cannot.
Q14. Passage 2 primarily challenges Passage 1 by:
  • A. Questioning whether Diamond's research was conducted rigorously
  • B. Arguing that the same geographic conditions produced different outcomes over time, suggesting that geography alone cannot explain historical divergence
  • C. Claiming that European power was actually the result of military rather than environmental advantages
  • D. Agreeing with Diamond's conclusion while disputing his evidence
Answer: B. Passage 2's key counterexample is the fertile crescent: same geography, different outcomes at different times. This directly challenges determinism — if geography caused outcomes, the same geography should produce similar outcomes consistently. A attacks methodology (not raised). C introduces military power (not in Passage 2). D is wrong — McCloskey disputes the conclusion, not just the evidence.
Q15. Diamond (Passage 1) would most likely respond to McCloskey's point about the fertile crescent by:
  • A. Conceding that culture and institutions are more important than geography
  • B. Arguing that his thesis concerns long-term patterns across millennia, not the relative fortunes of specific regions in particular centuries
  • C. Claiming that the fertile crescent's decline was caused by environmental degradation, not cultural factors
  • D. Agreeing that geographic determinism cannot explain European dominance
Answer: B. Diamond's framework (Passage 1) is about "history's broad patterns" — the long arc, not century-to-century fluctuations. He would defend by saying McCloskey's example operates on a shorter timescale than his thesis covers. A concedes the argument entirely — not what Passage 1 supports. C introduces environmental degradation — not in Passage 1. D contradicts Passage 1's entire thesis.
Q16. Both passages would most likely agree that:
  • A. Geography is the primary explanation for European dominance
  • B. Cultural factors fully explain why some societies accumulate more power than others
  • C. Understanding why some societies developed more power than others is a significant historical question
  • D. Environmental determinism is a discredited framework in modern historiography
Answer: C. Both passages are explicitly engaging with the same question: why did European societies come to dominate? They disagree on the answer but share the premise that it's an important question worth investigating. A is only Passage 1's view. B is only Passage 2's implied view. D overstates — Passage 2 critiques determinism but doesn't call it "discredited."
Passages for Q17–Q18
Passage 1: Research in positive psychology consistently finds that expressing gratitude improves wellbeing. Studies show that participants who wrote three positive things that happened each day for two weeks reported significantly higher life satisfaction, lower rates of depression, and improved sleep compared to control groups. These effects persisted for months after the intervention ended.

Passage 2: The positive psychology movement's emphasis on gratitude practices has been criticized for its inadequate attention to systemic factors. Psychologist Edgar Cabanas argues that encouraging individuals to cultivate gratitude can obscure structural causes of unhappiness — poverty, discrimination, poor working conditions — by framing distress as a failure of personal attitude rather than a reasonable response to unjust circumstances.
Q17. How does Passage 2 relate to the argument in Passage 1?
  • A. It provides additional evidence that gratitude practices improve wellbeing
  • B. It contradicts Passage 1's findings by showing that gratitude lowers life satisfaction
  • C. It challenges the value of gratitude interventions on ethical and political grounds, rather than disputing their psychological effects
  • D. It extends Passage 1 by exploring how gratitude practices can be applied to workplace settings
Answer: C. Crucially, Passage 2 does NOT say gratitude doesn't improve wellbeing — it says that even if it does, promoting it can have a harmful political effect (masking structural causes). This is an ethical/political challenge, not a psychological contradiction. B would require Passage 2 to dispute the study's findings — it doesn't. C captures this nuanced relationship precisely.
Q18. The author of Passage 1 would most likely respond to Cabanas' critique by:
  • A. Noting that the studies measured improvements in individual wellbeing and were not designed to address systemic social conditions
  • B. Conceding that gratitude practices are politically harmful and should not be recommended
  • C. Arguing that poverty and discrimination do not affect psychological wellbeing
  • D. Claiming that the two-week intervention in the study is sufficient to address structural inequality
Answer: A. Passage 1 is purely about individual psychological outcomes — life satisfaction, depression, sleep. The author would defend the research on its own terms: it measured what it measured (individual wellbeing) and didn't claim to address structural issues. B concedes too much — nothing in Passage 1 suggests the author would abandon their findings. C is an extreme claim not supported by Passage 1. D is a non-sequitur.
Relationship type: Complication & Extension — Q19 to Q22
Passages for Q19–Q20
Passage 1: Ecologists have documented the dramatic recovery of wolf populations in Yellowstone National Park following reintroduction in 1995. Within a decade, elk populations were reduced and redistributed, allowing vegetation along riverbanks to regenerate. Increased plant cover stabilized stream banks, reduced erosion, and ultimately altered the physical course of rivers — a phenomenon known as a trophic cascade.

Passage 2: While the Yellowstone trophic cascade is widely cited as a success story, ecologists caution against treating it as a universal template for rewilding. The cascade's magnitude depended on specific conditions: a large, unfenced park, a genetically diverse founding wolf population, and decades of overgrazed vegetation primed for rapid recovery. In smaller, more fragmented habitats — the context for most rewilding proposals worldwide — the same dynamics are unlikely to replicate.
Q19. How does Passage 2 relate to the argument in Passage 1?
  • A. It contradicts Passage 1 by arguing that wolf reintroduction harmed the Yellowstone ecosystem
  • B. It extends Passage 1 by describing additional trophic cascades in other national parks
  • C. It complicates Passage 1 by questioning whether the specific conditions that enabled the cascade can be replicated elsewhere
  • D. It confirms Passage 1's findings and calls for immediate wolf reintroduction worldwide
Answer: C. Passage 2 opens by calling Yellowstone "a success story" — it accepts Passage 1's findings. But it adds a caveat: those results depended on specific conditions not present in most rewilding sites. This is a classic complication: "yes, but only under these conditions." A contradicts what Passage 2 says. B is wrong — no other parks are described. D is the opposite of what Passage 2 argues.
Q20. The ecologists cited in Passage 2 would most likely caution against which of the following?
  • A. Studying trophic cascades in large national parks
  • B. Documenting vegetation recovery after wolf reintroduction
  • C. Assuming that wolf reintroduction will produce equivalent ecological results in smaller, fragmented habitats
  • D. Including genetic diversity as a consideration in rewilding programs
Answer: C. Passage 2 explicitly states that "in smaller, more fragmented habitats... the same dynamics are unlikely to replicate." The ecologists' caution is directly about assuming Yellowstone's results transfer to other contexts. A, B, and D are all things Passage 2 would support, not caution against.
Passages for Q21–Q22
Passage 1: Neurological research has established that bilingual individuals develop stronger executive function — the cognitive processes that control attention, task-switching, and inhibition — compared to monolinguals. Managing two languages simultaneously requires constant mental arbitration, which exercises and strengthens prefrontal cortex function over time.

Passage 2: The bilingual advantage in executive function has proven more difficult to replicate than initially believed. A 2020 review of 152 studies found that the advantage was robust only in populations with high bilingual engagement — individuals who used both languages daily across multiple domains. Passive bilinguals, who speak a second language but rarely deploy it, showed no significant executive function advantage, suggesting that frequency of use, not bilingualism per se, is the operative variable.
Q21. Passage 2 relates to Passage 1 most like a:
  • A. Full confirmation that strengthens the original finding
  • B. Complete refutation that shows the finding is false
  • C. Refinement that identifies the specific conditions under which the finding holds
  • D. Methodological extension that applies the finding to new populations
Answer: C. Passage 2 doesn't say the bilingual advantage doesn't exist — it says it's "robust only in" high-engagement bilinguals. This is a refinement: the original finding holds, but only under a specific condition (daily active use). B is too strong — Passage 2 doesn't say the advantage is false. A is wrong — Passage 2 complicates it. D is wrong — no new populations are introduced, conditions for the existing finding are specified.
Q22. Based on both passages, which conclusion is best supported?
  • A. Learning a second language always improves executive function regardless of how often it is used
  • B. Executive function differences between bilinguals and monolinguals are too small to be meaningful
  • C. Active, frequent use of two languages may be more important than bilingualism itself in producing executive function benefits
  • D. Neurological research has failed to establish any connection between bilingualism and brain function
Answer: C. Passage 1 attributes the benefit to "managing two languages simultaneously" (active use). Passage 2 confirms the advantage is present only with "daily use across multiple domains" and absent in passive bilinguals. Together, both passages converge on the idea that frequency of active use is the key mechanism — not simply "being bilingual." A overstates (ignores the passive bilingual caveat). B and D contradict both passages.
Relationship type: Methodological critique — Q23 to Q25
Passages for Q23–Q25
Passage 1: A widely cited 2018 study found that children who spent more than two hours per day on screens had lower cognitive test scores and thinner cortexes in areas associated with attention and impulse control. The researchers concluded that excessive screen time is causally linked to reduced cognitive development in children aged 9–10.

Passage 2: The 2018 study's causal conclusions are not warranted by its methodology. The study measured screen time and brain structure at a single point in time — a cross-sectional design that cannot establish causation. Children with pre-existing attention difficulties may be more drawn to screens, meaning the relationship could run in the opposite direction. Furthermore, the study did not adequately control for parenting style, household income, or sleep quality, all of which independently predict both screen use and cognitive outcomes.
Q23. Passage 2 primarily challenges Passage 1 by:
  • A. Presenting a different study that found no relationship between screen time and cognition
  • B. Arguing that the study's design prevents it from establishing the causal relationship it claims
  • C. Questioning whether cortex thickness is a valid measure of cognitive development
  • D. Suggesting that the study should have focused on teenagers rather than young children
Answer: B. Passage 2's entire argument is methodological: (1) cross-sectional design can't prove causation, (2) reverse causation is possible, (3) confounds weren't controlled. All of these challenge the causal claim, not the correlational finding itself. A introduces a different study — not in Passage 2. C questions the measure — not raised in Passage 2. D is not mentioned.
Q24. Which additional finding would most strengthen the argument in Passage 1 against the critique in Passage 2?
  • A. A survey showing that parents underestimate how much screen time their children have
  • B. A study showing that cortex thickness varies significantly by ethnicity
  • C. A longitudinal study tracking the same children over five years that found screen time at age 9 predicted lower cognitive scores at age 14, even after controlling for prior attention difficulties
  • D. An analysis showing that children in high-income families use screens more than children in low-income families
Answer: C. Passage 2's two main critiques are: (1) cross-sectional design can't prove causation, and (2) reverse causation is possible (attention problems → screens). C directly addresses both: a longitudinal design establishes temporal order (screens at 9 → outcomes at 14), and controlling for prior attention difficulties rules out reverse causation. A, B, and D don't address either of Passage 2's specific methodological objections.
Q25. Both passages would most likely agree that:
  • A. Screen time definitively causes reduced cognitive development in children
  • B. Screen time has no meaningful relationship with cognitive outcomes
  • C. The relationship between screen time and children's cognitive development is worth investigating scientifically
  • D. Cross-sectional study designs are insufficient for studying any health outcome
Answer: C. Passage 1 conducts and reports on the research as significant. Passage 2 critiques the methodology but clearly treats the question as important — otherwise why engage with it so carefully? Both passages presuppose that this is a question worth studying. A is only Passage 1's claim (and Passage 2 disputes it). B contradicts both. D overstates Passage 2's critique — it objects to the specific causal claim, not all cross-sectional research.

Part 1: Transitions — deep dive
~13–15 questions per test section
The core principle

Every transitions question has exactly one correct answer — the transition that accurately names the logical relationship between the two ideas. The wrong answers are grammatically acceptable but logically wrong. Your job is to classify the relationship, then match it to a transition word.

Students who guess based on "sound" get these wrong consistently. Students who classify first get them right almost every time.

The 3-step method (always follow this order)

1. Cover the answer choices. Read the two sentences around the blank.
2. Classify the relationship between the two ideas using the six types below.
3. Match to a transition from your memorized list for that type. Then confirm it fits.

The six relationship types — master list
1. Contrast / opposition
Idea B opposes or reverses idea A.
Words: however, nevertheless, yet, in contrast, on the other hand, despite this, even so, whereas, while, still
2. Cause & effect / result
Idea B follows from or is caused by idea A.
Words: therefore, thus, consequently, as a result, hence, accordingly, for this reason, so
3. Addition / elaboration
Idea B adds more information in the same direction as idea A.
Words: furthermore, additionally, moreover, in addition, also, similarly, likewise, what is more
4. Example / illustration
Idea B illustrates or specifies idea A.
Words: for example, for instance, specifically, in particular, notably, to illustrate
5. Concession / acknowledgment
Idea B admits a point that seems to undermine idea A, then moves forward anyway.
Words: admittedly, granted, of course, it is true that, to be sure, even though
6. Restatement / clarification
Idea B rephrases or clarifies idea A in different words.
Words: in other words, that is, namely, put differently, to clarify, in essence
The hardest trap — contrast vs. concession

Students confuse "however" (pure contrast) with "admittedly" (concession). The difference:

  • Contrast: A says X. B says not-X (or the opposite). The two ideas are simply opposed.
  • Concession: The writer acknowledges a point against their argument, but the main claim still stands. The sentence after the concession usually continues with "but" or "still" or "nevertheless."
Worked example — contrast
Early astronomers believed the universe was static and unchanging. _______, Edwin Hubble's 1929 observations of receding galaxies demonstrated that the universe is in fact expanding.

Relationship: The second sentence directly contradicts the first. → Contrast → "However"

Worked example — concession
_______ renewable energy sources like solar and wind are intermittent, advances in battery storage technology are making them increasingly viable for base-load power generation.

Relationship: The writer admits a weakness (intermittency) but then pivots to argue it doesn't undermine viability. → Concession → "Admittedly" or "Although"

Worked Q1. Which transition best completes the text?

High-altitude training increases red blood cell production, improving oxygen delivery to muscles. _______, many elite athletes spend weeks training at elevations above 2,000 meters before major competitions.
  • A. Nevertheless
  • B. In contrast
  • C. As a result
  • D. For example
Answer: C. Sentence 1: altitude training increases red blood cells → better oxygen delivery. Sentence 2: athletes train at altitude before competitions. This is a result — because of the benefit described in sentence 1, athletes do what sentence 2 describes. "As a result" names this cause-effect relationship. "Nevertheless" (contrast) is wrong — there's no opposition. "For example" is wrong — sentence 2 isn't an example of sentence 1; it's a consequence of it.
Worked Q2. Which transition best completes the text?

_______ the procedure carries a small risk of infection, patients who undergo the surgery report dramatically improved quality of life within six months.
  • A. Furthermore
  • B. Therefore
  • C. Although
  • D. For instance
Answer: C. The sentence structure is: [weakness acknowledged] + [positive outcome anyway]. This is a concession — the writer admits the infection risk but pivots to the quality-of-life improvement. "Although" introduces the conceded point before the main claim. "Furthermore" (addition) is wrong — the two ideas are in tension, not aligned. "Therefore" (result) is wrong — the quality-of-life improvement doesn't follow from the infection risk.

Part 2: Rhetorical Synthesis — deep dive
~3–4 questions per test section
What the question is actually testing

The SAT gives you 3–5 bullet-point notes from a "student's research" and asks you to combine them into a sentence that accomplishes a specific stated goal. The goal is always in the question stem: "emphasize a contrast," "illustrate a cause-and-effect relationship," "introduce the topic to an unfamiliar audience," etc.

The question is not asking which answer is most accurate or most complete. It's asking which answer most directly accomplishes the stated goal — even if it leaves out information from the notes.

The goal-first method (non-negotiable)

1. Read the goal sentence. Underline the key verb phrase: "emphasize the contrast," "explain the cause," "highlight the similarity," "introduce the subject," "support the claim that."
2. Before looking at the answers, think: what would a sentence that accomplishes this goal look like?
3. Eliminate any answer that doesn't do exactly that — even if it uses facts from the notes accurately.
4. Among survivors, pick the most direct and efficient one.

The four trap answer patterns
  • Trap 1 — Wrong goal: The answer is accurate but accomplishes a different goal than the one stated. (e.g., question asks for a contrast; answer presents a sequence.)
  • Trap 2 — Incomplete goal: The answer does part of the goal but leaves out a critical element. (e.g., question asks for a cause-and-effect chain; answer only describes the effect.)
  • Trap 3 — Too much: The answer crams in extra facts from the notes that are irrelevant to the goal, obscuring the target claim.
  • Trap 4 — Unsupported judgment: The answer makes a claim (e.g., "the most important," "proved definitively") not present in the notes.
Worked example
A student is researching the history of antibiotics and has taken these notes:

• Alexander Fleming discovered penicillin in 1928 after observing that mold killed bacteria in a petri dish
• Penicillin was not widely available until World War II, when mass production was developed
• Before antibiotics, bacterial infections like pneumonia and sepsis had mortality rates above 30%
• By 1950, penicillin had reduced pneumonia mortality to under 5%
• Antibiotic resistance is now considered one of the leading global health threats
Worked Q3. The student wants to illustrate the life-saving impact of penicillin using a specific before-and-after comparison. Which choice most effectively uses the notes to accomplish this goal?
  • A. Alexander Fleming's 1928 discovery of penicillin, which he made by observing that mold killed bacteria, eventually led to mass production during World War II.
  • B. Antibiotics have transformed medicine, though antibiotic resistance now threatens to undo those gains.
  • C. Before antibiotics, pneumonia killed more than 30% of patients; by 1950, penicillin had cut that rate to under 5%.
  • D. Penicillin is widely considered one of the most important medical discoveries of the twentieth century, saving millions of lives worldwide.
Answer: C. Goal: "illustrate the life-saving impact using a specific before-and-after comparison." C does exactly this: before (30% mortality) vs. after (under 5%). A covers discovery and production — relevant facts but wrong goal (the goal is impact, not history). B mentions resistance — also wrong goal and adds an unasked-for caveat. D makes a judgment ("most important") that isn't in the notes and doesn't use the before-after comparison.

Part 3: Add / Delete / Revise — deep dive
~2–3 questions per test section
Three distinct question subtypes
  • Should this be added? A sentence is proposed. Does it directly support the paragraph's main focus? Does it create a logical gap if omitted? If yes to both — add it. If the passage flows fine without it, or it introduces off-topic information — don't add it.
  • Should this be deleted? A sentence is bracketed. Does it directly serve the paragraph's purpose? If the passage loses essential information by removing it — keep it. If removing it makes the passage cleaner and more focused — delete it.
  • Which revision best accomplishes ___? A sentence is underlined. Four revised versions are offered. Pick the one that best accomplishes the stated purpose (introduce, clarify, emphasize, transition, etc.).
The two-question test for every Add/Delete decision

Question 1: What is the paragraph's main point? (State it in one sentence.)
Question 2: Does this sentence directly serve that main point?

If yes → keep or add. If no → delete or don't add. This eliminates all temptation to keep sentences just because they're interesting or true.

The common trap — "interesting but irrelevant"

The SAT almost always makes the sentence-to-delete interesting and factually related to the topic. Students keep it because it seems relevant. The test is whether it serves the specific paragraph — not the broader topic.

Worked example
The development of the printing press by Johannes Gutenberg around 1440 fundamentally transformed information distribution in Europe. Before the press, books were hand-copied by scribes, making them expensive and rare. [Gutenberg was born in Mainz, Germany, around 1400 and trained as a goldsmith before turning to printing.] After the press, books could be produced in large quantities at a fraction of the cost, enabling widespread literacy and fueling the Protestant Reformation and the Scientific Revolution.
Worked Q4. Should the bracketed sentence be kept or deleted?
  • A. Kept, because it provides helpful biographical context about the inventor of the printing press
  • B. Kept, because it explains how Gutenberg's goldsmith training contributed to the printing press's design
  • C. Deleted, because the paragraph focuses on the press's impact on information distribution, not on Gutenberg's personal history
  • D. Deleted, because it contradicts the claim that Gutenberg developed the press around 1440
Answer: C. Main point: how the printing press transformed information distribution. The bracketed sentence gives Gutenberg's birthplace, birth year, and occupation — none of which serve that main point. Notice that B is a trap: it implies a connection (goldsmith → printing press design) that is NOT actually stated in the sentence. D is wrong — there's no contradiction. The sentence should be deleted because it's biographical detail in a paragraph about societal impact.

Transitions — Q5 to Q14
Q5. Which transition best completes the text?

In the nineteenth century, most physicians dismissed the idea that invisible microorganisms could cause disease. _______, Louis Pasteur's germ theory experiments provided compelling evidence that bacteria were responsible for fermentation and putrefaction, reshaping medical understanding entirely.
  • A. However
  • B. Similarly
  • C. For this reason
  • D. In other words
Answer: A. Sentence 1: physicians dismissed germ theory. Sentence 2: Pasteur provided evidence that supported germ theory. These are opposed — contrast. "However" signals that despite the dismissal, evidence emerged. "Similarly" is wrong (implies agreement). "For this reason" is wrong (implies sentence 2 results from sentence 1 — it contradicts it). "In other words" is wrong (sentence 2 isn't a restatement).
Q6. Which transition best completes the text?

Deforestation removes trees that absorb carbon dioxide from the atmosphere. _______, large-scale forest loss significantly accelerates the accumulation of greenhouse gases and contributes to climate change.
  • A. Nevertheless
  • B. For example
  • C. Consequently
  • D. In contrast
Answer: C. Sentence 1 states a mechanism (trees absorb CO₂). Sentence 2 states the outcome of removing those trees (greenhouse gas accumulation). This is cause → effect. "Consequently" names that relationship. "Nevertheless" (contrast) and "In contrast" both imply opposition — wrong. "For example" would make sentence 2 an illustration of sentence 1, but it's actually a downstream consequence.
Q7. Which transition best completes the text?

The Roman Empire's road network facilitated military movement, trade, and communication across vast distances. _______, the postal relay system, known as the cursus publicus, allowed official messages to travel from one end of the empire to the other in a matter of days.
  • A. However
  • B. As a result
  • C. For instance
  • D. Therefore
Answer: C. Sentence 1 makes a general claim: roads facilitated communication across vast distances. Sentence 2 gives a specific example of that: the postal relay system allowed messages to travel quickly. This is a general claim → specific illustration. "For instance" names this relationship. "As a result" and "Therefore" (cause-effect) are wrong — the postal system isn't a consequence of roads; it's an example of their use.
Q8. Which transition best completes the text?

_______ some critics argue that social media has shortened attention spans and degraded public discourse, longitudinal studies of media consumption have not found consistent evidence of significant cognitive changes across age groups.
  • A. Furthermore
  • B. Therefore
  • C. Although
  • D. Similarly
Answer: C. Structure: [concern acknowledged] + [evidence does not support concern]. The first clause concedes a worry; the second says the data doesn't confirm it. This is a concession structure — "although" introduces the conceded point. "Furthermore" (addition) and "Similarly" (parallel) both imply the two ideas align — they don't. "Therefore" implies the evidence is caused by the critics' argument — wrong.
Q9. Which transition best completes the text?

Standard economic theory predicts that raising the minimum wage will cause employers to reduce their workforce to control costs. _______, a landmark 1994 study by economists Card and Krueger found that a minimum wage increase in New Jersey did not reduce fast-food employment compared to neighboring Pennsylvania.
  • A. In contrast
  • B. In addition
  • C. As a result
  • D. To illustrate
Answer: A. Sentence 1: theory predicts job losses from minimum wage increases. Sentence 2: a study found no job losses. These are opposed — contrast. "In contrast" names the opposition between theoretical prediction and empirical finding. "In addition" (addition) is wrong — the study contradicts, not supplements. "As a result" is wrong — the study wasn't caused by the theory. "To illustrate" is wrong — the study contradicts, not illustrates.
Q10. Which transition best completes the text?

The human brain continues developing well into a person's mid-twenties, with the prefrontal cortex — the region governing judgment and impulse control — among the last areas to fully mature. _______, neuroscientists and legal scholars have increasingly questioned whether adolescents should be held to the same criminal liability standards as adults.
  • A. Nevertheless
  • B. For example
  • C. In contrast
  • D. As a result
Answer: D. Sentence 1: the prefrontal cortex (judgment, impulse control) matures late. Sentence 2: scholars question whether adolescents should face adult criminal standards. The questioning in sentence 2 follows logically from the finding in sentence 1 — a cause-effect relationship. "As a result" names this correctly. "Nevertheless" would imply the questioning happens despite the brain finding — wrong; it happens because of it.
Q11. Which transition best completes the text?

Many ancient cultures developed written language independently of one another. The Sumerians developed cuneiform around 3200 BCE; the Egyptians developed hieroglyphics around the same time. _______, the Olmec civilization in Mesoamerica created its own distinct script centuries later.
  • A. However
  • B. Therefore
  • C. Similarly
  • D. In other words
Answer: C. The passage lists multiple civilizations that independently developed writing. The Olmec is another example of the same phenomenon — independent development. This is addition/parallel, and "similarly" signals that the Olmec case parallels the earlier examples. "However" implies a contrast — wrong. "Therefore" implies a result — wrong. "In other words" implies restatement — wrong.
Q12. Which transition best completes the text?

_______ coral bleaching events have become more frequent due to rising ocean temperatures, some reef systems have demonstrated a limited capacity for recovery when conditions temporarily stabilize.
  • A. Although
  • B. Furthermore
  • C. Consequently
  • D. For this reason
Answer: A. The sentence acknowledges a problem (bleaching is more frequent) but pivots to a hopeful note (some reefs recover). This is a concession: the problem is real, BUT recovery is possible. "Although" introduces the conceded point. "Furthermore" implies both ideas are aligned positively — wrong. "Consequently" and "For this reason" both imply the recovery is caused by the bleaching — wrong and logically reversed.
Q13. Which transition best completes the text?

James Watson and Francis Crick published the double-helix structure of DNA in 1953, drawing heavily on X-ray crystallography data produced by Rosalind Franklin. _______, Franklin received no credit in the original publication, and her contribution was largely unacknowledged during her lifetime.
  • A. As a result
  • B. In addition
  • C. Yet
  • D. Similarly
Answer: C. Sentence 1: Watson and Crick drew heavily on Franklin's data. Sentence 2: Franklin received no credit. Given the heavy reliance, one would expect credit — but the opposite happened. This is contrast/opposition with an implied "despite this." "Yet" captures this perfectly. "As a result" would mean non-credit resulted from reliance — logically wrong. "In addition" implies the two facts are complementary — wrong.
Q14. Which transition best completes the text?

The placebo effect demonstrates that a patient's expectation of improvement can produce measurable physiological changes — reduced pain, lower blood pressure, improved immune response. _______, some researchers now argue that the mind-body relationship is far more complex than classical biomedicine acknowledges.
  • A. In contrast
  • B. For instance
  • C. Accordingly
  • D. Admittedly
Answer: C. Sentence 1: expectation produces measurable physical changes (evidence). Sentence 2: researchers argue the mind-body relationship is complex (conclusion drawn from that evidence). The researchers' argument follows from the placebo data — cause-effect/result. "Accordingly" means "in a manner consistent with this" — exactly right. "For instance" is wrong — sentence 2 isn't an example of sentence 1. "Admittedly" implies concession — wrong.
Rhetorical Synthesis — Q15 to Q20
Notes for Q15–Q17
A student researching urban heat islands has gathered these notes:

• Urban heat islands (UHIs) occur when cities are significantly warmer than surrounding rural areas
• Dark surfaces like asphalt and rooftops absorb and retain solar heat more than vegetation does
• Cities typically have 15–35% less vegetation than rural areas of the same size
• UHIs can raise city temperatures by 1–7°C above surrounding rural areas
• Green roofs — rooftops covered with vegetation — can reduce local temperatures by up to 3°C
• A 2021 study found that cities with 20% more tree cover had significantly lower heat-related mortality
Q15. The student wants to explain the physical mechanism that causes urban heat islands. Which choice best accomplishes this goal?
  • A. Urban heat islands can raise city temperatures by 1–7°C and are associated with higher rates of heat-related mortality.
  • B. Because cities replace vegetation with heat-absorbing surfaces like asphalt and rooftops, they retain more solar energy and become significantly warmer than surrounding rural areas.
  • C. Green roofs and increased tree cover are two strategies that cities have adopted to reduce the effects of urban heat islands.
  • D. Urban areas are typically 15–35% less vegetated than rural areas of equivalent size, which has led researchers to study temperature differences between cities and their surroundings.
Answer: B. Goal: explain the physical mechanism. B chains the mechanism: removed vegetation → replaced with heat-absorbing surfaces → more solar energy retained → warmer city. This is a causal explanation. A describes the outcome (temperature rise, mortality) but not the mechanism. C describes solutions — wrong goal entirely. D describes vegetation loss and mentions research but doesn't explain the mechanism of heat retention.
Q16. The student wants to present a solution to urban heat islands and quantify its effectiveness. Which choice best accomplishes this goal?
  • A. Urban heat islands are a serious problem that requires immediate attention from city planners and policymakers.
  • B. Cities with less vegetation are warmer, which is why researchers have proposed numerous interventions over the past several decades.
  • C. Green roofs, which cover rooftops with vegetation, can reduce local temperatures by up to 3°C — a meaningful reduction given that heat islands typically raise city temperatures by 1–7°C.
  • D. The link between urban vegetation and temperature has been well established, suggesting that planting trees is beneficial for cities.
Answer: C. Goal: present a solution AND quantify its effectiveness. C names the solution (green roofs), explains it (vegetation on rooftops), and gives specific numbers (up to 3°C reduction, in context of 1–7°C typical range). A is too vague — no solution or quantity. B mentions "numerous interventions" but names none and quantifies nothing. D is vague ("beneficial") with no specific solution or number.
Q17. The student wants to argue that increasing urban tree cover has a measurable public health benefit. Which choice best accomplishes this goal?
  • A. Trees are valuable in cities because they reduce temperatures, improve air quality, and provide shade for pedestrians.
  • B. Urban heat islands have significant consequences for human health, particularly in cities with low vegetation levels.
  • C. A 2021 study found that cities with 20% more tree cover had significantly lower heat-related mortality, demonstrating that urban greening can save lives.
  • D. Because heat islands are caused by reduced vegetation, restoring trees to urban environments is a logical response to the problem.
Answer: C. Goal: argue that tree cover has a measurable public health benefit. C uses the 2021 study directly: 20% more trees → lower heat-related mortality. This is specific, measurable, and clearly a public health benefit. A mentions benefits but none are specifically "public health" and none come from the notes as a cited finding. B describes consequences but doesn't connect tree cover to health. D makes a logical argument but doesn't cite measurable evidence.
Notes for Q18–Q20

  • A. Print journalism is in crisis: newspapers are closing at a rapid rate, and local communities are losing their primary source of civic information.
  • B. US newspaper circulation has collapsed from a peak of 63 million in 1984 to under 20 million by 2022, while over 2,500 papers have closed since 2005.

Q19. The student wants to argue that the loss of local newspapers has consequences beyond just fewer news sources. Which choice best accomplishes this goal?
  • A. Local newspapers are closing at approximately two per week in the United States, a trend that has accelerated in recent years.
  • B. The decline of print journalism is largely driven by the shift of advertising revenue to digital platforms like Google and Meta.

This is exactly what "beyond" signals — democratic accountability, not just media access. A and D describe the closure trend (number of news sources) — the thing we're told to go beyond. B explains a cause of decline, not a consequence of it.
Q20. The student wants to introduce the topic of print journalism's decline to a reader unfamiliar with it, emphasizing the speed of the change. Which choice best accomplishes this goal?
  • A. The decline of print journalism has multiple causes, including the rise of online platforms and shifting advertising revenue.
  • B. Once the dominant source of news for millions of Americans, print newspapers have seen their audience shrink from 63 million daily readers in 1984 to under 20 million by 2022 — a collapse spanning less than four decades.
  • C. Studies linking newspaper closures to civic decline suggest that the loss of local journalism is more than just a business story.
  • D. Local newspapers are now closing at a rate of about two per week, and digital advertising revenue flows primarily to tech platforms rather than journalists.
Answer: B. Goal: introduce the topic to an unfamiliar reader AND emphasize speed of change. B opens with context for newcomers ("once the dominant source"), gives the full scope of decline (63M → 20M), and explicitly notes the timeframe ("less than four decades") to convey speed. A focuses on causes — not an introduction to the phenomenon. C assumes reader already knows about closure/civic decline. D presents two separate facts without orienting the reader to the topic.

Q21. The writer is considering whether to keep or delete the bracketed sentence. Should it be kept or deleted?

  • B. Kept, because it provides a chronological anchor that helps the reader understand the timeline of the reef's decline

  • A. Yes, because it reinforces the reef's importance and strengthens the argument for protecting it
  • B. No, because the paragraph focuses on the reef's ecological characteristics and climate threat, and economic data introduces a different focus

Passage for Q23–Q25

[A] Confirming its existence required decades of international collaboration, involving thousands of scientists from over 100 countries and billions of dollars in funding. Q24. The writer is considering deleting the sentence "The collider itself is located in a 27-kilometer tunnel beneath the French-Swiss border." Should it be kept or deleted?
  • A. Kept, because the geographic location of the discovery is important context for understanding its significance
  • B. Kept, because it provides technical detail that explains how the particle was discovered
  • C. Deleted, because the paragraph focuses on the scientific significance of the discovery, and the collider's physical location does not contribute to that focus
  • D. Deleted, because the information is inaccurate

The sentence gives a geography fact about the tunnel — true, related to CERN, but irrelevant to the scientific significance. The paragraph flows perfectly without it. Q25. The writer wants to revise the final sentence to more clearly state the broader importance of the discovery for physics. Which revision best accomplishes this?
  • A. The discovery confirmed the existence of a particle that Peter Higgs had predicted in 1964.
  • B. The Standard Model is the most successful theoretical framework in the history of physics.
  • C. By confirming the last unobserved particle predicted by the Standard Model, the discovery completed and validated physics' most comprehensive theory of the fundamental structure of matter.
  • D. Scientists celebrated the discovery, which had required billions of dollars and thousands of researchers to achieve.
Answer: C. Goal: clearly state the broader importance for physics. C does this precisely: it explains that this was the LAST unobserved particle, meaning the discovery COMPLETED the Standard Model — the most comprehensive physics theory. This is the "broader importance." A only restates the prediction fact. B describes the Standard Model but doesn't connect it to the discovery's specific importance. D focuses on the effort, not the scientific significance.
