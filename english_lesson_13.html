Concept 13 / 27Expression of Ideas
Rhetorical
Synthesis
You are given a set of research notes and asked to write a sentence that accomplishes a specific stated goal. The notes are raw material. The goal is the only thing that matters.
~2–3 per moduleTarget: 60 secRule: goal first, facts second
🔀
What is Rhetorical Synthesis?
Step 1
The format

The question gives you a bulleted list of research notes — typically 4–6 facts about a topic. Then it states a specific writing goal and asks which answer choice best accomplishes that goal.

Example goal: "The student wants to introduce the topic to an audience unfamiliar with it, emphasising its historical origins."

Example goal: "The student wants to compare the two methods using specific data from the notes."

Example goal: "The student wants to argue that Method A is superior to Method B."

The notes contain the raw facts. Your job is to find the answer that uses those facts to accomplish the stated goal — not the answer that uses the most facts, or the most accurate facts.

The most important thing to understand

Accurate ≠ correct. All four answer choices will use facts accurately drawn from the notes. But only one accomplishes the stated goal. Three of them use real facts for the wrong purpose.

This is why students miss these questions: they pick the answer that has the most facts, or the most precise facts, rather than the answer that does exactly what the goal says.

Read the goal first. Underline the key verb. The verb tells you exactly what to look for: "introduce," "compare," "argue," "emphasise," "explain the cause," "highlight the benefit," "describe the method."

⭐
The 4-step method
Step 2
1
Read the notes quickly. Skim for the key facts — don't memorise, just get a sense of what's there. 15 seconds maximum.
2
Read the goal carefully. Underline the verb and any key modifiers. "Introduce the topic to an unfamiliar audience" — verb: introduce. Modifier: unfamiliar audience. "Compare the two approaches using data" — verb: compare. Modifier: using data.
3
Before reading choices, think: what would an answer that accomplishes this goal look like? Rough mental template: "It would probably start with [X] and include [Y] and NOT include [Z]."
4
For each choice, ask one question: Does this accomplish the goal? Not: is it accurate? Not: does it use lots of facts? Just: does it do what the goal says? Eliminate all that don't. Among survivors, pick the most complete and direct.
⚠️
The 4 traps
Step 3
Trap 1 — most common
Most facts ≠ correct answer
One wrong choice packs in multiple accurate data points — students pick it because it feels comprehensive. But if it doesn't accomplish the goal, it's wrong. Goal beats data.
Trap 2
Accurate but wrong purpose
A choice that would perfectly accomplish a DIFFERENT goal — but not this one. "Introduce" ≠ "argue." "Compare" ≠ "explain why." Each answer uses facts for a specific purpose; match the purpose to the goal.
Trap 3
Missing the audience modifier
Goal: "introduce to an unfamiliar reader." Wrong answer: uses technical jargon without explanation. Right answer: defines terms, gives context. The audience described in the goal changes what counts as the correct approach.
Trap 4
Introduces information not in notes
Any answer that brings in facts not supported by the notes is wrong — even if those facts are true in general. The correct answer uses ONLY what the notes provide.

Worked examples
Step 4
Example 1 — Argue using evidenceMedium
Notes:
• Mangrove forests store 3–5 times more carbon per hectare than terrestrial tropical forests
• Global mangrove coverage has declined by 35% since 1980
• Mangrove restoration costs approximately $1,500–$12,000 per hectare
• Coastal communities protected by mangroves have 70% lower storm damage than unprotected ones
• One hectare of restored mangrove sequesters 1.5 tonnes of carbon annually
💭 Goal
Goal: "The student wants to argue that mangrove restoration is a cost-effective climate solution, using specific data from the notes." Key verb: ARGUE. Modifier: cost-effective + specific data. I need: an argument for restoration + cost + carbon data.
Which choice best accomplishes the goal?
  • A. Mangrove forests are important ecosystems that have been significantly reduced in coverage over recent decades, making their protection a priority for climate policy.
  • B. At a restoration cost of $1,500–$12,000 per hectare, mangroves protect coastal communities from storm damage while also providing habitat for marine species.
  • C. Given that restored mangroves sequester 1.5 tonnes of carbon per hectare annually — at a cost of as little as $1,500 per hectare — mangrove restoration offers a compelling return on climate investment compared to many conventional mitigation strategies.
  • D. Mangrove forests store 3–5 times more carbon than terrestrial tropical forests and have declined by 35% since 1980, making their loss a major driver of greenhouse gas emissions.
Answer: C. Goal: argue cost-effectiveness using data. C uses the cost ($1,500/ha) and the carbon sequestration (1.5t/ha/year) together to make an argument for value. A is general and doesn't argue using data. B uses cost data but argues storm protection, not climate cost-effectiveness. D uses carbon and decline data but doesn't make the cost-effectiveness argument.
Example 2 — Introduce to unfamiliar audienceMedium
Notes:
• CRISPR-Cas9 is a gene-editing technology developed in the early 2010s
• It uses a guide RNA to direct the Cas9 protein to a specific DNA sequence
• The Cas9 protein cuts the DNA at the target location
• CRISPR has been used to correct genetic mutations in trials for sickle cell disease
• Jennifer Doudna and Emmanuelle Charpentier won the 2020 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for developing CRISPR
💭 Goal
Goal: "introduce CRISPR to a general audience with no prior knowledge of the technology." Key verb: INTRODUCE. Modifier: general audience, no prior knowledge. I need: what it is, plain-language explanation, probably its significance. Do NOT assume the reader knows what RNA, Cas9, or sequencing means.
Which choice best accomplishes the goal?
  • A. CRISPR-Cas9 uses a guide RNA to direct the Cas9 protein to a specific DNA sequence, where it makes a precise cut — a mechanism that won its developers the 2020 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.
  • B. Developed in the early 2010s and recognised with a Nobel Prize in 2020, CRISPR-Cas9 is a gene-editing tool that allows scientists to locate and modify specific sections of DNA — a capability with potential applications ranging from treating genetic diseases to improving crops.
  • C. The sickle cell disease trials demonstrate that CRISPR has moved beyond the laboratory into clinical application, though questions about long-term safety and equitable access remain.
  • D. Jennifer Doudna and Emmanuelle Charpentier's Nobel Prize-winning work on CRISPR represents a milestone in molecular biology, enabling programmable genome editing at a resolution not previously possible.
Answer: B. Goal: introduce to a general audience. B defines what CRISPR is in plain language ("gene-editing tool that allows scientists to locate and modify specific sections of DNA"), gives its timeline (early 2010s), its recognition (Nobel), and its significance (treating diseases, improving crops) — all without assuming technical knowledge. A assumes the reader knows what RNA and Cas9 are. C assumes familiarity with CRISPR and pivots to a debate about it — not an introduction. D uses technical language ("molecular biology," "programmable genome editing," "resolution") unsuitable for a general audience.
Example 3 — Compare using dataHard
Notes:
• Traditional incandescent bulbs convert ~5% of energy into light; the rest becomes heat
• LED bulbs convert ~90% of energy into light
• Incandescent bulbs cost ~$1 each; LED bulbs cost ~$5–$8 each
• An LED bulb lasts approximately 25,000 hours vs 1,000 hours for incandescent
• Over a 25,000-hour lifespan, LEDs cost ~$30 in electricity vs ~$180 for incandescent
💭 Goal
Goal: "compare the long-term cost of LEDs vs incandescent bulbs to show LEDs are more economical." Key verb: COMPARE + show LEDs are more economical. I need: cost comparison over time + conclusion that LEDs are cheaper in the long run.
Which choice best accomplishes the goal?
  • A. LED bulbs are significantly more energy-efficient than incandescent bulbs, converting 90% of energy to light compared to just 5%, making them the environmentally superior choice.
  • B. Although LED bulbs cost $5–$8 compared to $1 for incandescent bulbs, LEDs last 25 times longer and use far less energy.
  • C. While LEDs cost more upfront ($5–$8 vs $1), their electricity cost over 25,000 hours is approximately $30 compared to $180 for incandescent bulbs — meaning LEDs are substantially more economical over their full lifespan.
  • D. LED technology has advanced dramatically, producing bulbs that outlast traditional incandescent options by a ratio of 25:1 while also delivering superior energy efficiency.
Answer: C. Goal: compare long-term cost + show LEDs cheaper. C does exactly this: acknowledges the higher upfront cost (acknowledges the objection), then uses the lifetime electricity data ($30 vs $180) to demonstrate long-term economy. A compares efficiency but doesn't make the cost argument. B mentions longevity and cost but doesn't use the electricity cost data to complete the economic argument. D mentions longevity and efficiency but makes no cost comparison.

Practice — 5 questions
Step 5
Practice 1
Notes:
• Deep-sea mining targets polymetallic nodules on the ocean floor
• These nodules contain manganese, cobalt, nickel, and copper
• Mining disturbs seafloor sediment, which can take thousands of years to resettle
• The Clarion-Clipperton Zone in the Pacific is the primary target area
• Species in the Clarion-Clipperton Zone are largely undiscovered and scientifically uncharacterised
Goal: The student wants to explain why deep-sea mining poses a particular scientific risk, using evidence from the notes.

Which choice best accomplishes the goal?
  • A. Deep-sea mining in the Clarion-Clipperton Zone extracts valuable minerals including manganese, cobalt, nickel, and copper that are essential to modern electronics manufacturing.
  • B. The sediment disturbance caused by deep-sea mining can persist for thousands of years, making the environmental damage extremely difficult to reverse.
  • C. Because the species living in target zones like the Clarion-Clipperton Zone are largely uncharacterised by science, mining-induced disturbances that take thousands of years to settle risk destroying biodiversity that has never even been documented.
  • D. Deep-sea mining involves extracting polymetallic nodules from the ocean floor, a process that disturbs sediment and impacts the marine ecosystem.
C. Goal: explain the particular SCIENTIFIC risk. C combines two specific facts from the notes — undiscovered species + thousands-of-years settlement — to explain why the risk is specifically scientific (destroying what we don't yet know). B mentions the sediment persistence but doesn't explain why that's a scientific risk. A explains economic value — not scientific risk. D is general and vague.
Practice 2
Notes:
• The Great Barrier Reef covers 344,000 km² off northeast Australia
• It supports 1,500 fish species and 4,000 types of mollusc
• Coral bleaching events have occurred in 1998, 2002, 2016, 2017, 2020, 2022, and 2024
• Ocean temperature increases of 1–2°C above average trigger bleaching
• The reef contributes AUD $6.4 billion to the Australian economy annually
Goal: The student wants to highlight the increasing frequency of bleaching events as evidence that the reef's health is worsening over time.

Which choice best accomplishes the goal?
  • A. The Great Barrier Reef supports extraordinary biodiversity — including 1,500 fish species and 4,000 types of mollusc — while contributing AUD $6.4 billion annually to the Australian economy.
  • B. Having experienced only two bleaching events in the seventeen years between 1998 and 2015, the reef has suffered five bleaching events in the nine years since 2016 — a pattern that strongly suggests its health is deteriorating as ocean temperatures rise.
  • C. Coral bleaching occurs when ocean temperatures rise 1–2°C above average, a threshold that is being crossed with increasing frequency due to climate change.
  • D. The Great Barrier Reef is a vital ecosystem and economic resource, but bleaching events driven by rising ocean temperatures represent a growing threat to its future.
B. Goal: highlight increasing FREQUENCY as evidence of worsening health. B uses the dates strategically — 2 events in 17 years vs 5 in 9 years — to demonstrate the acceleration. This directly accomplishes the goal of using frequency as evidence. C explains what causes bleaching but doesn't demonstrate the increasing frequency pattern. A uses biodiversity and economic data — irrelevant to bleaching frequency. D is vague and general.
Practice 3
Notes:
• Finland introduced a basic income pilot in 2017–2018 with 2,000 unemployed participants
• Participants received €560/month unconditionally
• Employment rates among participants were no lower than the control group
• Participants reported significantly higher wellbeing and lower stress than the control group
• Finland's broader welfare system remained unchanged during the pilot
Goal: The student wants to challenge the common claim that basic income reduces incentives to work, citing specific evidence from the pilot.

Which choice best accomplishes the goal?
  • A. Finland's basic income pilot provided 2,000 unemployed citizens with €560 per month and produced notable improvements in participants' reported wellbeing and mental health.
  • B. The Finnish pilot demonstrates that basic income can coexist with an existing welfare system without replacing it, suggesting it could complement rather than disrupt current support structures.
  • C. Contrary to the assumption that unconditional income reduces work incentives, Finland's pilot found that participants who received €560 per month worked at rates no lower than those in the control group.
  • D. Basic income pilots like Finland's provide valuable data on the economic and social effects of unconditional payments, informing ongoing policy debates around welfare reform.
C. Goal: CHALLENGE the work-incentive claim using specific evidence. C directly sets up the claim being challenged ("assumption that unconditional income reduces work incentives"), then cites the specific evidence (employment rates no lower). This is exactly the goal. A uses wellbeing data — the goal is about work incentives. B uses the welfare system data — not about work incentives. D is vague and doesn't cite specific evidence.
Practice 4 — Hard
Notes:
• The human brain contains approximately 86 billion neurons
• Each neuron can form up to 10,000 synaptic connections with other neurons
• Neural pathways strengthen with repeated use (synaptic plasticity)
• The brain can form new neural connections throughout life (neuroplasticity)
• Learning a new skill activates neuroplasticity and creates new pathways
Goal: The student wants to explain how repeated practice produces lasting improvement in a skill, drawing on the biological mechanism described in the notes.

Which choice best accomplishes the goal?
  • A. The human brain's 86 billion neurons, each capable of forming up to 10,000 connections, give the brain an extraordinary capacity for information processing and learning.
  • B. Each time a skill is practised, the neural pathways associated with it are used and strengthened through synaptic plasticity — the biological mechanism by which repeated activation makes connections more efficient and durable, producing the lasting improvement we recognise as mastery.
  • C. The brain's neuroplasticity means it can form new neural connections throughout life, making it theoretically possible to learn new skills at any age.
  • D. Learning a new skill activates the brain's neuroplasticity, demonstrating the extraordinary adaptability of the human brain and its capacity for lifelong development.
B. Goal: explain how REPEATED PRACTICE → lasting improvement via the biological mechanism. B specifically connects repeated practice → pathway use → synaptic plasticity (the mechanism) → lasting improvement (mastery). It traces the full causal chain. A discusses scale (86bn neurons) — not the mechanism of improvement. C explains neuroplasticity generally but doesn't connect it to repeated practice → improvement. D mentions neuroplasticity but is vague about the mechanism and doesn't explain the practice → improvement link.
Practice 5 — Hard
Notes:
• Kelp forests can grow up to 30–60 cm per day under ideal conditions
• They provide habitat for over 1,000 species
• Kelp forests absorb CO₂ and have been proposed as a climate mitigation tool
• Rising ocean temperatures are reducing kelp forest coverage globally
• Kelp forest area has declined by 40% in some regions since the 1950s
Goal: The student wants to present kelp forests as both ecologically valuable and environmentally threatened, to motivate conservation action.

Which choice best accomplishes the goal?
  • A. Kelp forests, which can grow up to 60 cm per day, are among the fastest-growing organisms on Earth and have potential applications in carbon sequestration.
  • B. Rising ocean temperatures are reducing kelp forest coverage globally, threatening the more than 1,000 species that depend on these ecosystems for habitat.
  • C. Kelp forests support over 1,000 species and absorb carbon dioxide at rates that make them a promising climate solution — yet rising temperatures have already caused a 40% decline in coverage in some regions, making conservation an urgent priority.
  • D. The 40% decline in kelp forest coverage since the 1950s represents a significant loss of marine habitat and underscores the need for immediate action to address ocean warming.
    • C. Goal: present BOTH ecological value AND threat, to motivate conservation. C does both: value (1,000+ species, CO₂ absorption, climate solution) AND threat (40% decline, rising temps) AND explicitly calls for conservation. B only covers the threat. A only covers the value (growth rate + carbon). D only covers the threat and decline — doesn't establish the ecological value that makes conservation worth pursuing.
